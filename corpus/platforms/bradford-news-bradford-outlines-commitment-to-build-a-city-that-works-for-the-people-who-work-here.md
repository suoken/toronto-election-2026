Title: BRADFORD OUTLINES COMMITMENT TO BUILD A CITY THAT WORKS FOR THE PEOPLE WHO WORK HERE
Published: 2026-10-21
Issue category: Toronto Mayoral Campaign 2026

Brad Bradford visited Atlantic Packaging in Scarborough as they sign on as an Anchor Partner to Bradford’s Toronto Youth Employment Challenge, and said the best thing City Hall can do for a business is take care of the people who show up to work there every day, and make Toronto a stable, predictable place to operate

TORONTO, ON | Today Brad Bradford toured Atlantic Packaging Products in Scarborough, a family-owned company that has been making packaging in this city since 1945, and laid out what a Bradford administration will do for the businesses that employ the people of Toronto.

Atlantic Packaging and company owner David Granovsky also signed on as the second Anchor Partner of Bradford’s Toronto Youth Employment Challenge, a direct challenge from the Mayor's office to Toronto's employers to create 15,000 permanent, full-time youth jobs. Atlantic joins Borrowell and its CEO Andrew Graham as successful local business leaders committed to creating more good jobs for young people starting their career here.

"When you talk to a company like Atlantic Packaging, you learn fast what a business actually needs from City Hall," said Bradford. "It isn't a ribbon cutting. It's a city where their people can get to work on time, where their kids are safe in the park after school, where the roads aren't falling apart and the tax bill isn't a surprise. Take care of the people who work here, and the business takes care of itself."

The people who work at Atlantic live across Toronto. They commute to Progress Avenue from Scarborough, North York, East York and beyond. They want what every family in this city wants: a safe neighbourhood, a commute that doesn't eat their evening, roads that don't wreck their car, good schools, parks their kids can play in, and a city that doesn't ask for more every year while delivering less.

Bradford's plan delivers on the 3 things that matter most to those families: crime, congestion and cost.

-
Crime. 1,000 net new front line police officers by the end of a first term, on patrol in neighbourhoods and at subway stations. A city that is not safe is not free. A worker finishing a late shift should not have to think twice about the walk to the bus.

-
Congestion. Improving transit safety and reliability, including a 15-minute service guarantee on the TTC, a single point of accountability for construction with published timelines and real consequences, and a commitment to get Scarborough moving again. Every hour stuck in traffic is an hour a worker doesn't get back with their family and an hour a business pays for.

-
Cost. A property tax freeze in year 1 that applies to businesses as well as homes, water bills cut up to 25%, and the first full, independent, line by line review of City spending since 2011. Stable. Predictable. A business can plan around that.

Businesses do not ask City Hall for favours. They ask for predictability. An employer needs to know what the tax bill will be next year, how long a permit will take, and that the City will do what it says it will do. Red tape is a tax on ambition. A Bradford administration will treat every dollar and every day a business spends dealing with City Hall with the respect it deserves.

Bradford has already put a plan on the table for the people who make things in this city. In August, standing on the workshop floor at Brave Leather, a Toronto maker of belts and leather goods hit by the 50% U.S. tariffs, he committed a Bradford administration to a permanent tariff-response table at City Hall, so manufacturers and workers are in the room the day a shock hits and have one front door into every federal and provincial support program. He committed to real property tax relief, not a deferral, for roughly 3,500 industrial businesses and 30,000 small businesses across the city. He committed to getting approvals done in months, not years, for manufacturers expanding or modernizing in Toronto, and to putting the City’s own purchasing power behind Toronto-made and Ontario-made goods, with the receipts published. A company like Atlantic Packaging, which makes its products here and ships them across the border, is exactly who that plan is for.

The province has never given the City of Toronto more money than it has over the past three years. NDP Mayor Chow still raised property taxes nearly 20%, the largest increase in the city's history. If she's re-elected she'll raise them another 20% at least. Every one of those increases lands on the employers who create jobs here and on the families who work for them. Food bank visits are up 340% since 2019, 4.1 million visits last year alone. Where did the money go?

NDP Mayor Chow stood on the debate stage and declared that Toronto has reached its full potential. Bradford rejects that. A city where workers lose hours of their week to the worst commutes in North America, and where employers budget for another tax increase every year, is not at its full potential. We're not even close.

"I'm a city planner by training. I know what makes a city competitive, and it's the same thing that makes it livable," said Bradford. "The question a company asks before it invests here isn't about incentives. It's whether their people can build a good life in this city. Right now that answer is harder than it should be. Toronto isn't broken. It's stuck. We're on the cusp of change. Change is within our grasp. But to change the city, we need to change the mayor."
