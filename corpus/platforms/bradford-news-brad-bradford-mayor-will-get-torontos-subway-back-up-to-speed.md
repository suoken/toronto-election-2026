Title: BRADFORD WILL GET TORONTO'S SUBWAY BACK UP TO SPEED
Published: 2026-10-20
Issue category: Congestion

TORONTO, October 20, 2026 | If you ride the subway, you know the feeling. The train leaves the station, picks up speed, and then slows to a crawl for no reason you can see. It creeps along for a minute or 2, then speeds up again. That is a slow zone.

Brad Bradford today committed that a Bradford administration will set a standard of zero slow zones on Toronto's subway, publish the count every month, and pay for the track reconstruction the system has been waiting on through the modernized budget he announced on October 1. Bradford made the announcement near Davisville Station in Toronto–St. Paul’s as part of his “25/7” tour of the entire city during the final week of this campaign.

A slow zone, or what the TTC calls a reduced speed zone, is a stretch of track where inspectors have found a problem and trains are ordered to slow from their normal 40 to 65 km/h to as little as 15 km/h until the track is repaired and inspected again. Each one adds up to three minutes to a trip, by the TTC's own estimate. Some are gone in a few days. Others sit for weeks or months, because the equipment needed to fix them is out of service or because the repair was temporary and the problem came back.

As of October 14, the TTC lists 13 of them on the subway. 11 are on Line 1, the Yonge-University line, which carries more than 650,000 riders a day and is the busiest rapid transit line in Canada. In five of them, trains are held to 15 km/h. Three have no repair date at all.

This is not new. It has been the story of the entire Chow administration. The TTC imposed 296 slow zones between January 2023 and September 2024, hit 34 at once in the summer of 2024, and has sat at about a dozen ever since. Three years and three budgets later, there has been no noticeable improvement. And riders have never been asked to give up more of their subway to get there: early weeknight closures and weekend shutdowns are now routine, people replan their nights out and their weekends around a subway that isn't open, and the slow zones keep coming.

"Your time is the one thing City Hall can't give back to you," said Bradford. "Right now the subway crawls at 15 kilometres an hour between Eglinton and Davisville, between Bloor and Rosedale, between Royal York and Islington. Imagine if the speed limit on our roads was changed to 15 km/hr because we didn’t maintain them properly? Allowing this to happen is disrespectful to transit riders.”

“The TTC's official position is that there will never be zero slow zones. I don't accept that,” said Bradford. “Boston cleared more than 200 slow zones in 12 months because they set the target at zero. A target of a dozen gets you a dozen. I'm setting the target at zero and I'm going to publish the number every month so you can hold me to it."

City Hall and the TTC promised a subway that ran at full speed for the FIFA World Cup. For four weeks, it did. During the tournament the count fell to two, the lowest in three years. But within weeks, it was climbing again. By August 25, the count stood at 18. Transit analyst Steve Munro has documented that a regular quarterly track inspection was deferred so its results would not trigger new slow orders just before the games.

"They pretended to fix the subway when the world showed up to Toronto, then let all the people who pay for it deal with the problems after," said Bradford. "That tells you everything about whose commute matters at City Hall. Olivia Chow stood on the debate stage and declared that Toronto has reached its full potential. On Line 1, that potential is 15 kilometres an hour."

The cause is well documented. The oldest outdoor sections of Line 1 and Line 2 still sit on wooden ties and ballast rather than a concrete base, so the track shifts with the weather and the same five stretches go back under slow orders every season. The work car fleet needed to repair them was pulled from service in April after hydraulic spills, even as spare subway cars sit in TTC yards. The same work car shortage is why the TTC's own report says the rollout of cellular service in the tunnels is at risk and has slipped into 2027. And $37.3 billion of the TTC's own 15-year capital plan, 69% of it, has no funding behind it, while more than $1 billion of the City's 2025 capital budget sat unspent.

A Bradford administration will:

-
Set a zero slow zone standard for the subway, adopted by the TTC board in the first 100 days, with every slow order carrying a published reason and a published repair date. No more "TBD."

-
Publish a monthly slow zone report showing the number of zones, how long each has been in force, and the total hours riders have lost to them, by line.

-
Order an independent track condition audit, public within the first 100 days, setting out what can be repaired and what has to be rebuilt, and the state of the work car fleet.

-
Launch a Subway Track Renewal Program to rebuild the chronic outdoor sections on a concrete base (direct fixation track) so the slow zones stop coming back, starting in the 2027 capital budget.

-
Renew the TTC's work car fleet by converting spare subway cars into work cars now, so the shortage ends in months rather than years, buying the TTC its own track inspection equipment so it stops renting and waiting in line behind other cities, and getting the delayed rollout of cellular service in the tunnels back on schedule.

-
Publish an annual closure calendar a year ahead, so crews get the windows to make permanent repairs and riders can plan around them, with replacement buses given priority on the road.

-
Never defer a track inspection for an event again. Inspections run on schedule and the results are published, whatever is on the calendar.

The plan is paid for inside the fiscal framework Bradford announced on October 1: a real separation of the operating and capital budgets, with long-lived infrastructure like subway track financed over its useful life under the tightest debt rules in the country, rather than paid for in cash out of this year's property taxes while the work waits. The operating budget balances every year. No new taxes. No service cuts.

"There are two ways to beat slow zones. You get disciplined about track repair, or you spend the capital to rebuild the worst stretches on a concrete base so they stop coming back. I'm going to do both," said Bradford. "Toronto pays cash up front for 50-year assets out of this year's taxes, which is why $37 billion of the TTC's repair plan has no money behind it and a billion dollars sat unspent last year. Every province does this differently. So does Toronto Hydro. Modernize the budget and the track gets rebuilt now, not never."

"The province has never sent the City more money, and NDP Mayor Chow still raised property taxes nearly 20%, the largest increase in the city's history. If she's re-elected she'll raise them another 20% at least. You are paying more and getting less, and you feel it every time the train slows to a crawl," said Bradford. "The Mayor has made her peace with less. I have not. Toronto isn't broken. It's stuck. We're on the cusp of change. Change is within our grasp. To change the city, we need to change the mayor."

Toronto votes on October 26.
