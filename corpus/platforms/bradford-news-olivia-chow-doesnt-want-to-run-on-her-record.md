Title: OLIVIA CHOW DOESN'T WANT TO RUN ON HER RECORD
Published: 2026-09-22
Issue category: Toronto Mayoral Campaign 2026

At tonight's Toronto Region Board of Trade debate, the Mayor was upset that Brad Bradford talked about the challenges Torontonians face every day. Of course she was.

TORONTO, ON | At tonight's Toronto Region Board of Trade mayoral debate, Olivia Chow made one thing clear: she doesn't want to run on her record. During the debate, Brad Bradford talked about what Torontonians are living through, in the scrum, the Mayor said she was upset that he was raising the city's challenges at all.

“I'm not going to stop talking about what people in this city are going through because it makes the Mayor uncomfortable," said Bradford. "The parents lining up at the food bank know what's happening. The commuters stuck on the Gardiner know what's happening. The families opening a bigger tax bill every year know what's happening. The Mayor's problem isn't that I'm talking about it. Her problem is that it's true."

Of course she's upset. For the Mayor's campaign to work, she has to convince Torontonians that what they're feeling every day isn't real. That the commute isn't that bad. That the tax bill isn't that high. That the food bank lines aren't that long. That this is as good as it gets.

She's said as much. At the Daily Bread debate, when candidates were asked whether Toronto is living up to its full potential, Olivia Chow raised her paddle and said yes. That's the status quo in one word. She looks at this city and thinks it's good enough.

Torontonians don't have that luxury. In 2023, Olivia Chow promised property tax increases would be "modest." Then she delivered a 9.5 per cent increase, then a 6.9 per cent increase, then an election-year 2.2 per cent increase she only got that low by drawing $1.7 billion from the city's reserves. That's nearly 20 per cent compounded, almost $700 more a year on the average home.

It happened while the province gave Toronto more help than it ever has. The 2023 New Deal is worth roughly $9 billion to the city over 10 years, including taking the Gardiner Expressway and the Don Valley Parkway off the city's books. More money than ever, higher taxes than ever, and a city that works worse than ever. Food bank visits hit a record 4.1 million last year, up 340 per cent since 2019. Torontonians sit through the worst commutes in North America. People don't feel safe at Yonge-Dundas or in Union Station. Where did the money go?

Tonight Bradford also committed to freeze property taxes for one year, for homes and businesses alike, while the first independent, line by line audit of city spending since 2011 finds out where Torontonians' money is actually going. The freeze is paid for out of the roughly $100 million City Hall budgets every year and never spends.

"The Mayor says Toronto has reached its full potential. I don't accept that. We're not even close," said Bradford. "Toronto isn't broken. It's stuck. And stuck is a choice. On October 26, Torontonians can choose change: a city that's safe, a city you can afford, a city that moves. A Toronto we can be proud of."
