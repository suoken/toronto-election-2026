Title: Bradford commits to start subway service at 6 am for next year’s marathon, releases 7-point Get Toronto Moving plan
Published: 2026-10-18
Issue category: Congestion

TORONTO, ON, October 18, 2026. This morning in downtown Toronto, mayoral candidate Brad Bradford announced that he would implement common-sense measures to get Toronto moving, including starting subway service two hours earlier at 6 a.m. on the day of the marathon.

More than 31,000 runners from over 75 countries came to Toronto this weekend for the TCS Toronto Waterfront Marathon, joined by thousands of volunteers, families and supporters, all headed for the downtown core early on a Sunday morning. The City of Toronto told them to leave the car at home and take transit. The problem is: the subways didn’t open until 8 am, so attendees were stuck without a serious transit option.

The TTC's own service notice for the weekend states that "subway service does not start until 8 a.m." Guess what else starts at 8 a.m.? The marathon. And runners and volunteers need to be there hours earlier. So most of them took a vehicle, dodging the major road closures needed for the marathon. And they’ll have to take a vehicle back.

Their only TTC option was the night bus system. Meanwhile, the City closed Lake Shore Boulevard from 5 a.m., closed Bathurst, Bloor and Yonge from 7 a.m., and diverted more than 2 dozen bus and streetcar routes from 4 a.m. It could figure out how to do all of that, but it could not open the subway two hours earlier.

"Everyone at City Hall knew the date of the marathon for a year. Everyone knew 31,000 people were coming. Opening the subway at 6 a.m. on marathon morning is common sense, and the Mayor or her staff didn’t think it was important," said Toronto mayoral candidate Brad Bradford. “When I’m mayor, we’ll start subway service at 6 a.m. on the day of the marathon to give people better options.”

Bradford said marathon morning follows the same pattern as Mother’s Day weekend, the 4th busiest traffic day of the year. That weekend saw a string of major events. The Blue Jays played three home games against the Angels at Rogers Centre. The Toronto Tempo played their first-ever WNBA game Friday night at a sold-out Coca-Cola Coliseum. Toronto FC hosted Inter Miami at BMO Field on Saturday in front of 44,828. And on Sunday morning, 24,000 runners lined up for the Sporting Life 10K down Yonge Street, with closures on Yonge, Bay, Bayview, Front and Mill. City Hall’s answer was to close the Don Valley Parkway from the 401 to the Gardiner for the entire weekend, shut down Line 2 from Jane to Kipling and take out the Gardiner on-ramp at British Columbia Road and Lake Shore, all at the same time. Then a crash near Jameson blocked the westbound Gardiner at noon on Saturday and the whole west end seized up.

“One bad weekend is a mistake. This is a pattern,” Bradford said. “NDP Mayor Chow has made her peace with a city that shows up for the event and forgets the people trying to get to it. I haven’t.”

"The TTC ran 6 a.m. Sunday service for the entire Pan Am Games in 2015. It runs the subway until 3 a.m. on New Year’s Eve, for free. It can be done. It just wasn't. That's the difference between a city that plans and a city that's stuck,” said Bradford.

THE GET TORONTO MOVING PLAN

Toronto drivers lost about 100 hours to traffic last year, more than the year before and almost as much as New York. Bradford calls it the time tax: the bedtime story you missed and the shift you couldn't take. A Bradford administration will act on seven practical measures to get the city moving:

-
Major event transit plans. Binding TTC and Metrolinx service plans published 30 days ahead of every marathon, Leafs, Raptors and Jays game and major concert, so the subway is open when the crowds arrive and neighbourhoods aren't paralyzed.

-
Fewer closed lanes during construction. Align noise bylaw timing with road occupancy permits so lane work happens overnight and capacity is back for the morning rush.

-
Faster streetcars. Full transit signal priority, automated switches and 500 metre stop spacing to cut streetcar travel times by 40%. Toronto's streetcars average 10.8 km/h. European cities run at 19. A streetcar that moves stops backing up every car behind it.

-
Smart traffic signals. A city-wide network of adaptive signal timing across all 2,500 intersections that responds to live traffic, replacing controllers that in places are two decades old and short of spare parts.

-
Smarter bike lanes. Maintain the downtown network and add new routes on quiet parallel streets, ravines and multi-use trails, not by removing driving lanes on busy arterials.

-
Technology-enabled pothole detection. Install Camera-based detection on existing City fleet vehicles, with repairs ranked by data, not requiring residents to report them all themselves.

-
Platform edge doors at every TTC subway station. An investment that will deliver fewer delays, safer platforms, more reliable service across the network.

"None of this is science fiction. Signal priority, adaptive traffic lights, overnight construction, platform doors. Other cities chose to move years ago. Toronto chose to stall," Bradford said. "The technology exists, the money exists, and the need is obvious to anyone who took a night bus to the start line this morning. What's been missing is a mayor who treats your time like it matters. That's the change I'm offering."

Olivia Chow stood on the debate stage and declared that Toronto has reached its full potential. Bradford rejects that. "Toronto isn't broken. It's stuck. And stuck is a choice," he said. "We're on the cusp of change. Change is within our grasp. On October 26, to change the city, we need to change the mayor."
