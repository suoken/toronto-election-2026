Title: TORONTO’S WATER STAYS PUBLIC. THE LAW SAYS SO.
Published: 2026-10-21
Issue category: Toronto Mayoral Campaign 2026

TORONTO, October 21, 2026. Bradford for Mayor spokesperson Laryssa Waler issued the following statement in response to today’s release from TCEU Local 416 and incumbent members of City Council:

“Let’s start with what today’s release leaves out. Provincial law bans private ownership of Toronto’s water. Under the Water and Wastewater Public Corporations Act, as amended this May, shares in a municipal water corporation can only be held by a municipality, the Province or the Government of Canada. Not an investor. Not a pension fund. Not a private company. Private ownership is prohibited in the statute itself. Brad Bradford’s plan goes further: the City is the sole shareholder, and a City bylaw bars private investment of any kind. Toronto’s water stays 100% public.

“The union’s own legal opinion all but concedes it. Its strongest argument is that a future government could one day change the law. That’s true of every law in Ontario. By that logic, nothing this city owns is safe, from Toronto Hydro to the TTC. Toronto Hydro has been a City-owned corporation since 1999, it borrows to build, and nobody calls it privatized.

“This isn’t a hand-off to the province either. Under Brad’s plan, City Council appoints the board and the City holds every share, the same structure Toronto already uses for Toronto Hydro. The people who operate and maintain our water system do excellent work, and they’ll keep doing the same jobs under the same collective agreements, which carry over under the provincial framework.

“What this release really defends is the status quo. The average family in this city pays $1,118 a year for water and wastewater, up 75% since 2009, and that bill went up in each of the last 3 years under NDP Mayor Chow. Toronto Water is sitting on about $2 billion in reserves and underspent its budget by nearly $400 million last year. That’s residents’ money, collected for pipes that still haven’t been built. The 15 incumbent councillors and NDP Mayor Chow lining up to keep it that way isn’t news. It’s what stuck looks like.

“On debt, Toronto Hydro borrows to build. So does every province and every major water utility in the country. Nobody pays cash for a house on move-in day. Brad’s plan pays for 100-year pipes over their useful life, and it cuts every water bill in Toronto by 25%. That’s about $300 a year for the average household, automatically, with no application and not one service cut.

“It’s the same pattern across this whole term. The province has never given the City more money, and NDP Mayor Chow still raised property taxes nearly 20%, the largest increase in the city’s history. If she’s re-elected, she’ll raise them another 20% at least. Food bank visits are up 340% since 2019, 4.1 million visits last year alone. Where did the money go? Olivia Chow stood on the debate stage and declared that Toronto has reached its full potential. Brad rejects that. We’re not even close.

“Toronto isn’t broken. It’s stuck. We’re on the cusp of change, and change is within our grasp. To change the city, we need to change the mayor.”

BACKGROUND

Ownership. The Water and Wastewater Public Corporations Act, 2025, as amended by Bill 98 (passed May 14, 2026), provides that shares in a municipal water corporation may only be held by a municipality, the Province of Ontario, the Government of Canada or an agent of any of them. Private ownership is expressly prohibited. Brad Bradford has committed that City bylaws will additionally bar any private investment in the utility.

Governance. Under the Bradford plan, City Council appoints an independent, skills-based board, sets upfront guidance on rates, and the City is the sole shareholder. The same model is used for Toronto Hydro, whose rates are approved by the Ontario Energy Board.

Employees. Existing employment contracts and collective agreements carry over to the new utility under the provincial framework.

The numbers. Average household water and wastewater bill: $1,118 a year (City of Toronto 2026 Water and Wastewater Rate Budget), up 75% since 2009. Toronto Water underspent its 2025 budget by nearly $400 million. Water and wastewater reserves have grown from $141 million in 2009 to about $2 billion. Toronto Water carries no debt.
