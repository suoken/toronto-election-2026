Title: BRADFORD RELEASES PLAN FOR FIRST 100 DAYS: 5 PRIORITIES, STARTING WITH PUBLIC SAFETY
Published: 2026-10-22
Issue category: Toronto Mayoral Campaign 2026

TORONTO, ON | This morning in Don Valley West, Brad Bradford sat down with a group of mothers to talk about the things they think about every day. Whether their kids are safe walking to school, riding the subway or on the playground. Whether they'll make it home in time for dinner. Whether their kids will find a first job and a first home in the city they grew up in. Then he released his plan for the first 100 days of a Bradford administration: five priorities, each with concrete steps, a mechanism and a timeline.

“The moms I sat down with today aren't asking for miracles. They're asking for a city that works,” said Bradford. “Job #1 is public safety. Because the data tells us we're not safe enough, and a city that is not safe is not free. Then we get Toronto moving, we find out where the money went, we fix how the budget is built and we put this city's young people to work. Change starts on day one.”

THE FIRST 100 DAYS

1. Public safety. Auto theft is up. Assaults are up. Break and enters are up. Home invasions are on the rise. Half a million more people have moved to Toronto and there are no more police officers on the street. People don't feel safe, and the numbers say they're right. In week one, Bradford will call in the TTC and the Chief of Police and work with them to deliver a transit safety strategy that puts a police officer at every subway station. He will take the seat on the police board that’s reserved for the mayor by law. He will work with the province and Toronto Police Service to begin the recruitment that gets Toronto to 1,000 net new front line officers by the end of a first term, with the first recruits in the pipeline before the 100 days are out, and open talks with the province on a Toronto campus of the Ontario Police College so the city can train its own officers again. He will personally carry Toronto's demand for bail reform to the Premier and the Prime Minister. And he will formally ask the province for more voluntary treatment options and a mandatory addiction treatment law for the most severe cases, with real safeguards, while beginning work on at least 100 recovery house beds in Toronto.

2. The Scarborough LRT and transit that works. Bradford will personally carry the business case for the Scarborough Eglinton East LRT to the Premier and the Prime Minister in his first 100 days. The plan exists. The route is drawn. The environmental assessment is done. What's been missing is a mayor who fights for it. He will begin removing the red bus lanes on Kingston Road, Morningside Avenue, Kennedy Road and Midland Road that were handed to Scarborough in place of the LRT it was promised. He will direct the TTC to deliver a plan to get rid of subway slow zones and put the 15-minute service guarantee in place. And he will name a single point of accountability for construction across the city, with published timelines and real consequences for projects that drag on for years. The time tax is the mom who misses bedtime because the streetcar sat in traffic. Nobody voted for it, and nobody gets those hours back.

3. A line by line review of City Hall spending. On day one, Bradford will order the first full, independent, line by line review of City Hall spending since 2011, with the results made public. The province has never given the City of Toronto more money than it has over the past 3 years. NDP Mayor Chow still raised property taxes nearly 20%, the largest increase in the city's history. If she's re-elected she'll raise them another 20% at least. Food bank visits are up 340% since 2019, 4.1 million last year alone. Where did the money go? $11 million on software licences for people who no longer work for the city. $5 million on recycling bin stickers. Nobody is minding the books. A Bradford administration will treat every dollar families send to City Hall with the respect it deserves.

4. A modern budget. Bradford's first budget, tabled within the first 100 days, will begin modernizing the City's budget with a true separation of operating and capital spending, the way Ontario did years ago and Prime Minister Mark Carney does. NDP Mayor Chow came into office with a $1.8 billion hole in the budget and is leaving with a $1.6 billion hole, with no plan to close it. His administration’s first budget will freeze property taxes for homes and businesses, start reducing water bills by up to 25%, about $300 a year for the average household, and bring the elimination of the land transfer tax on homes below $1.1 million to Council, about $18,000 back on closing day for a family buying their first home. Families don't sit at the kitchen table talking about capital budgets. But they expect the person running a government larger than four provinces to understand how one works.

5. More private-sector jobs for youth. Youth unemployment in Toronto is at 17%, four points higher than the national average. On his first day in office, Bradford will launch the Toronto Youth Employment Challenge and set a public goal of 5,000 new private-sector youth jobs in year 1 and 15,000 by the end of the term, with 30% of placements reserved for young people facing the steepest barriers. He will personally recruit 10 to 15 CEOs as founding Anchor Partners. Over the past week, Bradford has already recruited the CEOs of two leading Toronto employers, Atlantic Packaging and Borrowell, to serve as anchor partners.

“I'm a city planner by training. I know how cities are supposed to work, and I know what it takes to get City Hall moving,” said Bradford. “None of this requires a year of study. It requires a decision. Residents will see a credible plan for crime, a credible plan for congestion and a credible plan for cost in the first 100 days. Not platitudes. Numbers, dates and the names of the people responsible.”

Olivia Chow stood on the debate stage and declared that Toronto has reached its full potential. Bradford rejects that. A city where parents drive kids who used to take the bus to school is not our full potential. The worst commutes in North America is not our full potential. A generation of young people doing the math on whether to stay is not our full potential.

“Toronto isn't broken. It's stuck. And for three years, the people running this city have made their peace with that. I have not,” said Bradford. “We're on the cusp of change. Change is within our grasp. But to change the city, we need to change the mayor. In 4 days, Toronto gets to choose. And on day one, we get to work.”
