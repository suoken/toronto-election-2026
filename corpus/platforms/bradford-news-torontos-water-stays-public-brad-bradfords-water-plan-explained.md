Title: Toronto's Water Stays Public: Brad Bradford's Water Plan Explained
Published: 2026-10-18
Issue category: Toronto Mayoral Campaign 2026

Summary

Under Brad Bradford's water plan, Toronto's water system stays 100% publicly owned. Ontario law allows shares in a municipal water corporation to be held only by a municipality, the Province of Ontario, the Government of Canada or an agent of one of them. A Toronto bylaw would also bar private investment of any kind, and the City of Toronto would be the sole shareholder.

Brad Bradford, candidate for Mayor of Toronto in the October 26, 2026 election, proposes moving Toronto Water into a City-owned utility, run the way Toronto Hydro has been run since 1999. The plan cuts water bills by 25%, about $300 a year for the average household, with no service cuts - as explained in the September 28 release.

Mayor Olivia Chow's campaign has described the plan as privatization. The law, the bylaw and the ownership structure all say otherwise. The Bradford campaign's view is that the claim is meant to shift attention away from Toronto's rising water bills and the roughly $2 billion in reserves collected from residents.

What does Brad Bradford's water plan do?

The plan cuts every Toronto water bill by 25% on the first $1,500 of charges, about $300 a year for the average household. The cut is automatic, with no application, and no water services are reduced.

Today the average household pays $1,118 a year for water and wastewater, according to the City's 2026 Water and Wastewater Rate Budget. That bill is up 75% since 2009 and has risen in each of the last three years. About two thirds of each bill goes to capital rather than to delivering water.

In 2024 the system cost $909 million to run, including amortization, and billed $1.32 billion. The water reserves have grown from $141 million in 2009 to about $2 billion, and Toronto Water underspent its 2025 budget by nearly $400 million. The cut is paid for by ending this overcharge, not by raising other taxes.

How the utility would work:

-
The City of Toronto is the sole shareholder.

-
Rates are set by an independent, skills-based board appointed by City Council, within guidance set by Council.

-
The utility borrows to build long-lived pipes and plants and recovers the cost over their 50- to 100-year life, instead of collecting it all up front.

-
Toronto Water employees keep the same jobs under the same contracts; existing collective agreements carry over under the provincial framework.

-
Brad Bradford has written to Ontario's Minister of Municipal Affairs and Housing, Rob Flack, asking that the Residential Tenancies Act rent-reduction mechanism cover water rate cuts, so renters benefit too.

Can Toronto's water utility be privatized under Brad Bradford's plan?

No. Three separate protections keep Toronto's water utility in public hands, and each would have to fail for private ownership to happen. It would be 100% public, by law and by bylaw.

-
Provincial law. Section 9 of Ontario's Water and Wastewater Public Corporations Act, 2025 says shares of a water corporation cannot be issued to anyone other than a municipality, the Province of Ontario, the Government of Canada or an agent of one of them. Existing shareholders cannot sell or transfer shares to anyone else. Bill 98, passed May 14, 2026, added these limits to guarantee 100% public ownership.

-
City bylaw. Brad Bradford has committed to a Toronto bylaw barring private investment of any kind in the utility. Repealing it would take a public vote of City Council.

-
Sole public shareholder. The City of Toronto owns 100% of the utility on behalf of Toronto taxpayers, as it owns Toronto Hydro.

Under the Act, core service assets cannot be sold off unless they are declared surplus. Water quality stays regulated under the Safe Drinking Water Act, 2002, as it is today.

A private investor, pension fund or foreign company cannot buy shares in Toronto's water utility under this framework. Calling the plan privatization describes something the law does not allow.

Answers to the most common objections

"A future government could change the law." Any law can be amended, including the laws that govern Toronto Water today. Under the current system, water is a City department with no statutory ban on privatization at all. The utility model adds a provincial share restriction and a City bylaw that do not exist now. Privatizing it would take a new provincial law and a Council vote to repeal the bylaw, both in public.

"The Act lets an 'agent' of government hold shares." Some municipalities have asked the Province to define "agent" more tightly. In Toronto the question does not arise: the City is the sole shareholder, and the City bylaw bars private investment of any kind.

"Highway 407 was public and then went private." The 407 was sold under a law that allowed its sale. The Water and Wastewater Public Corporations Act expressly prohibits private ownership, so the same step would require changing the law first.

"A dividend to the City is just a hidden tax." Toronto Hydro has paid the City a dividend for decades without being called privatized. The water dividend is about $45 million in 2027, building toward about $125 million, while residents' bills fall by about $300 a year.

"Borrowing will drive rates up." Rates today include a large charge for pipes not yet built. Financing long-lived assets over their life lets today's bill reflect today's costs. Rates are set by a Council-appointed board within Council's guidance.

"If the utility defaults, a lender could seize the pipes." Under the Act, core water and wastewater assets cannot be sold unless Council declares them surplus. The utility borrows the way Toronto Hydro has since 1999.

"The union's legal opinion says the City can't bind the Province." The opinion, commissioned by CUPE Local 416 from Goldblatt Partners, argues that no City bylaw can stop a future provincial law. That is true of every arrangement the City has, including Toronto Water today, which sits under provincial statute with no ban on privatization at all. The utility model adds a statutory share restriction and a City bylaw that do not exist now.

Where does this model already work?

Publicly owned utility corporations are a common way for Canadian cities to deliver water and power, and none of the examples below are privatized.

-
Toronto Hydro: owned by the City of Toronto since 1999. It borrows to build, charges what service costs and pays a dividend to the City.

-
EPCOR, Edmonton: 100% owned by the City of Edmonton.

-
Enwin Utilities, Windsor; Utilities Kingston; Halifax Water: publicly owned utilities delivering water.

-
Peel Region: transitioning to a water and wastewater public corporation now, under the same provincial Act.

-
Calgary: an independent expert review panel recommended a utility model after the 2024 Bearspaw feeder main failure.

Describing Toronto's plan as privatization implies each of these cities sold its water. None of them did.

Why is Olivia Chow's campaign calling it privatization?

The Bradford campaign's view is that the privatization claim shifts the debate away from Toronto's water bills, which have risen every year of Mayor Chow's term. A debate about water rates puts the City's own numbers in front of voters.

Those numbers include:

-
An average household bill of $1,118 a year, up 75% since 2009.

-
About two thirds of each bill going to capital, much of it for pipes and plants not yet built.

-
Reserves of about $2 billion, up from $141 million in 2009, collected from residents through their water bills.

-
An underspend of nearly $400 million in Toronto Water's 2025 budget.

Privatization is a powerful word in a water debate, and voters are right to care about it. That is why the facts matter: the provincial law, the City bylaw and the City's sole ownership all rule it out.

Brad Bradford: "It's a ridiculous attack from Mayor Chow's campaign which is out of ideas and out of gas. Under my plan, Toronto's water stays 100% publicly owned. Not 99%. Not public with an asterisk. 100%, by law and by bylaw."

Frequently asked questions

Is Brad Bradford privatizing Toronto's water? No. The utility would be 100% owned by the City of Toronto, and Ontario law limits ownership to governments.

Who could own shares in the utility? Only a municipality, the Province of Ontario, the Government of Canada or an agent of one of them, under section 9 of the Water and Wastewater Public Corporations Act, 2025. In Toronto, the City would be the only shareholder.

Could the City sell the utility later? Not to a private buyer under current law. Shares can only be transferred to another government, and a City bylaw would bar private investment.

How much would my water bill drop? About 25% on the first $1,500 of charges, roughly $300 a year for the average household.

Do renters benefit? Brad Bradford has asked the Province to extend the Residential Tenancies Act rent-reduction mechanism to water rate cuts so renters see the savings.

What happens to Toronto Water employees? They keep their jobs under the same contracts and collective agreements.

Who sets water rates? An independent, skills-based board appointed by City Council, working within guidance set by Council.
Is the board unelected and unaccountable? The board is appointed by City Council, works within guidance Council sets, and can be replaced by Council. Toronto Hydro, the TTC and Toronto Community Housing are governed the same way today.

Will Doug Ford control Toronto's water rates? No. Under the Water and Wastewater Public Corporations Act, 2025, setting rates is a duty of the water corporation itself. In Toronto that means a board appointed by City Council, working within guidance set by Council, with the City as sole shareholder. The Province sets the legal framework and regulations for water utilities, as it already does for Toronto Water today.

Will Toronto's water safety be put at risk? No. The same provincial safety laws that protect Toronto's drinking water today continue to apply, including the Safe Drinking Water Act, 2002 and the Ontario Water Resources Act. The same treatment plants, standards, inspections and Toronto Water staff stay in place. Financing pipes and plants over their life also lets the utility repair and replace ageing infrastructure sooner, instead of waiting until the cash is saved.

Is this how Toronto Hydro works? Yes. Toronto Hydro has been a City-owned corporation since 1999 and pays a dividend to the City.
