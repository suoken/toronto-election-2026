Title: BRADFORD SHARES PLAN TO RESTORE THE PROMISE OF TORONTO FOR TORONTO’S YOUTH
Published: 2026-10-16
Issue category: Cost of Living

TORONTO, ON | This morning at the offices of Borrowell, a Toronto-founded fintech company, Brad Bradford sat down with CEO Andrew Graham and a group of students and young professionals to talk about what it takes to start a career, buy a first home and build a life in Toronto. They were joined by the President & CEO of Youth Employment Services (YES), Timothy Lang. Their conclusion is that the city is making it harder than it should be at every step.

Youth unemployment in Toronto is at 17%, four points higher than the national average. Across the country, the share of young people holding a job fell last year to its lowest level since 1998. Toronto's own Youth Cabinet found that 74% of young people face real barriers to employment. Add record rents, a land transfer tax no other Canadian city charges, and the worst commutes in North America, and the result is a generation doing the math on whether to stay or leave.

"Toronto has Canada's biggest tech hub and the country's largest financial centre in the same few square kilometres. Almost no city in the world has both," said Bradford. "We should be competing with New York, London and Singapore for every ambitious 24-year-old on the continent. Instead we're driving our own out. Not because they stopped loving Toronto. Because they stopped believing it's a place where they can build a good life. I won't accept that."

Bradford's plan to create opportunity for Toronto youth has 4 parts. As mayor, he will:

-
Launch the Toronto Youth Employment Challenge: a direct challenge from the Mayor's office to Toronto's employers to create 15,000 permanent, full-time youth jobs by the end of his term, 5,000 in year one. Bradford will personally recruit 10 to 15 CEOs as founding Anchor Partners, publish every pledge on a public portal with a quarterly leaderboard, and reserve 30% of placements for young people facing the steepest barriers. Employers fund the jobs. The City funds only the coordination, at under $750,000 a year. Boston's mayor delivered more than 10,500 youth hires in 2025 this way, and New York's CEOs have hired more than 65,000 young people since 2021.

-
Eliminate the municipal land transfer tax on the value of a home below the average Toronto price, $1.1 million. Under Bradford's plan, up to two thirds of homes sold in Toronto change hands with no city land transfer tax, about $18,000 back at the threshold. Most first-time buyers purchase condos, so for most of them the tax disappears entirely. It's funded with money the City already collects, and not one service is cut.

-
Clean up the city and make it safe, starting at Union Station, the front door of the country: a police station inside Union and another at Yonge-Dundas, published cleaning and safety standards, a visible police presence at every subway station, 1,000 more front line officers, and real help for people in a mental health crisis, including more voluntary and mandatory treatment options for people whose addiction has taken over their lives.

-
Build a City Hall that competes: a 15 minute transit service guarantee, a single point of accountability for construction with published timelines, faster permitting, and the first full, independent, line by line review of City spending since 2011.

"The CEO deciding where to locate the next 200 jobs isn't asking about our tax incentives. The question is whether her people can get to work easily, afford a home and feel safe walking to the subway," said Bradford. "What makes a city competitive is the same thing that makes it livable. Quality of life is the whole game, and right now Toronto is losing it."

The province has never given the City of Toronto more money than it has over the past three years. NDP Mayor Chow still raised property taxes nearly 20%, the largest increase in the city's history. If she's re-elected she'll raise them another 20% at least. In those same three years, food bank visits climbed to 4.1 million a year, up 340% since 2019. Where did the money go? Yesterday, 11 days before the election, the Mayor matched Bradford's 15,000 youth jobs commitment and tied it to a transit line she has not secured a dollar to build. A job on a project that isn't funded isn't a job.

"Toronto isn't broken. It's stuck. And nobody is more stuck than a young person who can't get started," said Bradford. "We're on the cusp of change. Change is within our grasp. On October 26, this city can choose to be the best place in the world to start a career, buy a first home and build a life. Not someday. Now. But to change the city, we need to change the mayor."
