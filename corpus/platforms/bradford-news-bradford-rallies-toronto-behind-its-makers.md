Title: BRADFORD RALLIES TORONTO BEHIND ITS MAKERS: “LET’S ALL DO OUR PART. I’LL START WITH MINE.”
Published: 2026-08-27
Issue category: Toronto Mayoral Campaign 2026

As new tariffs hit the things Toronto makes, Bradford puts his own campaign to work for local businesses, and lays out the change at City Hall that would match the effort

TORONTO, ON | Five days after 50 per cent U.S. tariffs took effect on roughly $20 billion of Canadian goods, mayoral candidate Brad Bradford stood on the workshop floor of Brave Leather, a Toronto maker of belts and leather goods, and called on Torontonians to stand together behind the city’s workers, shopkeepers, farmers and manufacturers, committing his own campaign to lead by example.

The new tariffs reach products previously protected under CUSMA and land directly on what Toronto makes: clothing, furniture, cement, dairy, wine and sporting goods.

The week since has only escalated: Washington has threatened to raise tariffs on Canadian cars and trucks to 50 per cent, and on Tuesday the federal government announced matching counter-tariffs on $27.6 billion of U.S. goods effective September 8, alongside $7.5 billion in new support for affected workers and businesses.

“This isn’t about pointing fingers. It’s about bringing this city together,” said Bradford. “Every consumer has a choice, and it takes a bit of work from each of us. Check the label. Pick the Ontario produce. Choose the shop around the corner instead of the click online from somewhere else. The people who invested in Toronto, who built their businesses here and hire here, need to see the love right now. Give it to them.”

Bradford said he isn’t waiting for election day to do his own part. His campaign has committed to:

-
Spotlight Toronto makers, shops and manufacturers regularly as this tariff war continues, using the platform of this race to put local businesses in front of the whole city. Today’s visit to Brave Leather, whose products sit squarely in the newly tariffed categories, is the first stop.

-
Buy Toronto and buy Canadian for everything the campaign itself purchases, wherever it’s available, from signs to sandwiches.

-
Invite Torontonians to send in the local businesses they love at info@bradford26.ca, so the campaign can share them and keep the spotlight moving.

-
Sit down with affected manufacturers, workers and business owners across the city in the coming weeks, so the response to this shock is built with the people living it.

“I’ve got a platform in this race, and the most useful thing I can do with it right now is use it to help the people who make things in this city,” said Bradford. “Canadians helping each other has always been bigger than politicians. But leadership means going first.”

Bradford also laid out the first steps on how he would match that effort at City Hall. As Mayor, he will:

-
Stand up a permanent City of Toronto tariff-response table with manufacturers, workers and industry representatives, in the room the day a shock hits, not weeks later. The table will also serve as one front door connecting Toronto businesses to the new federal and provincial tariff supports, so no employer is left to navigate them alone.

-
Deliver real property tax relief, not deferral, for every year the trade war is ongoing: 25 per cent relief for roughly 3,500 industrial businesses, helping employers weather the storm and keep people on payroll. Bradford first proposed this in a motion at City Council in February 2025, but it was voted down by Mayor Chow and her allies.

-
Get approvals done in weeks, not years, for manufacturers expanding or modernizing Toronto facilities.

-
Put the City’s full purchasing power behind Toronto- and Ontario-made goods: implement the Buy Ontario Act’s new municipal procurement requirements ahead of schedule, go further wherever trade rules permit, and publish where the City’s procurement dollars actually go. If we’re asking every family to check the label, City Hall should show its receipts too.

-
Partner with the Province to put Buy Ontario manufacturing supports to work for Toronto companies.

-
Give residents an honest accounting of the city’s reserves and a plan to rebuild them.

Bradford acknowledged the honest starting point for that work. The city enters this trade shock with its reserve funds drawn down by $1.7 billion to balance an election-year budget, the largest such drawdown in Toronto’s history, with the 2027 budget already opening roughly $1.1 billion short, and with the centrepiece of City Hall’s 2025 tariff response, an industrial property tax deferral, expired since last November, nine months before this round arrived.

Bradford’s case is that the city’s problem is not its people but its pace: Toronto enters this storm stuck, with permits measured in years, reserves spent before the rain came, and a tariff program that lapsed before the tariffs arrived. Change, he argues, is how the city gets unstuck.

“We can’t change what Washington does. But we can change how ready this city is for it. Change how City Hall spends, change how fast it moves, change who it shows up for. That’s what October 26th is: the day Torontonians get to make the change themselves,” said Bradford.

“Toronto isn’t broken. It’s stuck,” added Bradford. “On October 26th we can get it unstuck, and build a city we can all be proud of.”
