Title: BRADFORD WILL FREEZE PROPERTY TAXES FOR ONE YEAR WHILE WE FIND WHERE THE BILLIONS WENT.
Published: 2026-09-22
Issue category: Cost of Living

At tonight's Toronto Region Board of Trade debate, Bradford commits to a one-year freeze and the first independent, line by line review of city spending since 2011

TORONTO, ON | At tonight's Toronto Region Board of Trade mayoral debate, Brad Bradford announced that as mayor he will freeze property taxes for one year, for homes and businesses alike, while an independent, line by line review finds out where Torontonians' money is actually going.

Right now, nobody can answer that question. The City of Toronto hasn't had an independent, line by line look at its spending since 2011. In the 15 years since, the city's operating budget has grown by billions of dollars. The Mayor doesn’t care. She doesn’t think it’s important because in her view, this is as good as it gets. Toronto has reached its full potential. Brad Bradford rejects that idea. But this isn’t the first time Bradford and Chow see things differently.

In 2023, Olivia Chow promised property tax increases would be "modest." Then she delivered a 9.5 per cent increase, then a 6.9 per cent increase, then an election-year 2.2 per cent increase she only got that low by drawing $1.7 billion from the city's reserves. That's nearly 20 per cent compounded, almost $700 more a year on the average home.

It happened while the province sent Toronto more help than ever before. The 2023 New Deal is worth roughly $9 billion to the city over 10 years, including taking the Gardiner Expressway and the Don Valley Parkway off the city's books. More money than ever, higher taxes than ever, and a city that works worse than ever. Food bank visits hit a record 4.1 million last year, up 340 per cent since 2019. Torontonians sit through the worst commutes in North America. Where did the money go?

Now the Mayor is promising to hold increases to “around” the rate of inflation. She promised "modest" last time too.

"Every family in this city has had to sit down at the kitchen table and figure out exactly where their money goes. City Hall should have to do the same," said Bradford. "Olivia Chow has raised your property taxes nearly 20 per cent and she still can't tell you where the money went. So we're going to stop. No more increases until we open the books, read every line, and show Torontonians what we find."

The freeze is already paid for. A 2 per cent property tax increase raises about $100 million, and City Hall already has that room. It has it every year. In 2025, for the part of the budget that property taxes pay for, the city budgeted about $5.6 billion and spent about $5.5 billion, $100 million less than it planned. In 2024, that gap was $130 million.

Every year, City Hall sets your property taxes as if it will spend every dollar it budgets. Every year, it doesn't. Bradford's fiscal plan budgets for what the city actually spends and uses about $100 million of that recurring room to pay for the freeze. That means no property tax increase and no cuts to front-line services that people depend on.

"City Hall is taxing you for money it doesn't even spend," said Bradford. "Everyone in this room knows no business would go 15 years without an audit, and no business would survive budgeting like this. That's not a revenue problem. That's a management problem."

The freeze covers the City Tax Rate of every property in the city, homes and businesses alike. Toronto is competing with every major city in North America for jobs and investment, and the businesses that employ Torontonians have been paying more and getting less too. A year of certainty on property taxes tells every employer in this city that City Hall is finally getting its own house in order.

The line-by-line review will be led by an independent group of experts and former city leaders and will cover every division, agency and program. The goal is to protect and improve frontline services while finding the waste, the duplication and the outdated programs. Once the review is done, any future property tax increase will be kept below the rate of inflation.

"The Mayor says Toronto is living up to its full potential. I don't accept that. We're not even close," said Bradford. "Toronto isn't broken. It's stuck. And stuck is a choice. On October 26, Torontonians can choose change: a city that's safe, a city you can afford, a city that moves. A Toronto we can be proud of."
