Title: BRADFORD AT TORONTO’S FRONT DOOR: THE WORLD ARRIVES IN FOUR DAYS, AND CITY HALL HASN’T CLEANED UP FOR IT
Published: 2026-09-10
Issue category: Clean City

Days before the Prime Minister’s Canada Investment Summit, Bradford commits to a police sub-station at Union Station and says the investment case for Toronto is the city itself

TORONTO, ON | Four days before the first ever Canada Investment Summit brings the world’s largest investors to Toronto, mayoral candidate Brad Bradford stood overlooking Union Station with a simple message. Billions of dollars in global investment are looking for cities where they can answer a single question: is this somewhere my employees would want to live? Right now, he said, City Hall has left Toronto unable to answer it.

“When a company CEO picks a city to invest in, he or she considers a lot of things. Tax rates are on the list. But the top of the list, every time, is one question: is this somewhere my employees would want to live,” said Bradford. “And right now, we can’t look those executives in the eye and give an unconditional yes. That has to change, and on October 26 it can.”

There’s no better place to see it than Union Station. More than a quarter of a million people pass through it every day, more than any other transportation hub in Canada, in a building the City of Toronto owns. It’s a National Historic Site, built in an era when Toronto wanted the world to know it was going somewhere: a block-long colonnade on Front Street, a Great Hall four storeys high, the names of cities across the country carved into its stone. The day it opened in 1927, the Prince of Wales crossed the Atlantic to cut the ribbon, with the British Prime Minister at his side and Prime Minister Mackenzie King there to receive them.

And for the century since, it’s the room where Toronto begins. First jobs. First days in Canada. Students in September, soldiers coming home, kids on the way to their first Jays game. The Great Hall can still stop a first-time visitor in their tracks, Bradford said. The trouble is everything on the way to it: the grime, the doorways, the smell every commuter recognizes and nobody at City Hall will name. None of it, he said, is the fault of the crews who scrub that building every night. They’re working as hard as anyone in Toronto. It’s the standard the building’s owner has decided to live with.

“They opened Union Station in 1927 with a prince and two prime ministers. That’s how much this city’s front door mattered. It’s still where new lives begin. Your first job. Your first day in Canada. But now the welcome is disorder,” said Bradford. “That’s not on the crews who work miracles in that station every night. That’s the standard City Hall decided to accept. That’s what a stuck city looks like.”

The Canada Investment Summit, September 14 and 15, will gather sovereign funds, institutional investors and chief executives behind a stated ambition of $1 trillion in investment over five years. Bradford wants the summit to succeed. But Toronto is competing for every dollar against Montreal, Calgary and Vancouver, and against New York, London and Singapore, and nobody hands the host city the prize. The Prime Minister and the province are doing their parts, he said, while the host city’s Mayor has spent three years leaving the basics undone.

“The Mayor’s going to be at that summit getting her picture taken and her face on TV. That’s not the job. The job was over the past three years when she served as Mayor,” said Bradford. “Toronto doesn’t need a photo op participant. It needs a mayor who rolls up their sleeves and gets the work done.”

Bradford’s plan for the station, and the city around it, is already public. Today he added to it: as mayor, he will establish a Toronto Police substation at Union Station, a permanent, visible police presence inside the busiest building in the country, working alongside the station’s security and the TTC.

It joins the TTC Safety Plan he has already put on the table, which includes:

-
Sworn officers at every subway station.

-
Safety and cleanliness standards for every station, published so riders can see exactly how their station is doing.

-
Real platform edge doors, starting with the full height pilot the TTC’s own study recommended.

-
And a money back guarantee when the TTC leaves riders standing fifteen minutes past the promise.

The rest of the answer to the investor’s question is just as concrete: mental health outreach teams working with police, so a person in crisis gets help and a commuter gets to work safely; advocating for increased voluntary and mandatory treatment options for individuals suffering from the health crisis of severe drug addiction; public targets for maintenance of parks and public spaces; and one front door for permits, with the fee refunded when the city misses its own deadline.

“A quarter of a million people a day deserve to walk through that station and feel what a visitor felt in 1927: this is a city that has its act together,” said Bradford. “Police in the building. Standards on the wall. A front door Toronto is proud of. None of that is complicated. It’s just the job.”

“Toronto isn’t broken. It’s stuck,” said Bradford. “On September 14, the world comes to look at Toronto. On October 26, Torontonians decide what the world sees next. That’s the change on the ballot.”
