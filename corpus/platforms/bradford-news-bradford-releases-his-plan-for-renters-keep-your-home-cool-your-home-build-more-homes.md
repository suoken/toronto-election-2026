Title: BRADFORD RELEASES HIS PLAN FOR RENTERS: KEEP YOUR HOME. COOL YOUR HOME. BUILD MORE HOMES.
Published: 2026-10-05
Issue category: Toronto Mayoral Campaign 2026

Bradford sets out a three-part plan for the half of Toronto that rents: enforce the renoviction rules the City already has, deliver a maximum heat standard with a cool room in every rental home, and give non-profit builders clear rules and early money to build. These build on his commitments to lower water bills for all residents, including renters, and save first-time homebuyers $18,000.

TORONTO, ON | Brad Bradford today set out a plan for the nearly 560,000 households that rent in Toronto, almost half the city. Four in 10 of them already spend more than 30% of their income keeping a roof over their heads. Bradford’s plan will make life more affordable for renters while protecting them from renovictions and extreme heat.

Under NDP Mayor Olivia Chow, property taxes went up nearly 20%, starting with 9.5%, the largest increase in the city’s history, pushing rent prices higher. Renters got the bill. They did not get the results.

The City passed a renoviction licensing by-law in July 2025 and still cannot tell you how many renovictions it has stopped, because nobody publishes the results. 271,000 rental units sit in buildings with no air conditioning. Last summer the City’s air conditioner program delivered 489 units against 1,414 applications, turning away two-thirds of people who asked for help. Council has directed a maximum heat by-law for June 2027, and the Mayor’s own staff report says 90% of the landlords the City consulted intend to pass the cost on to tenants. She has no answer for that. And too many non-profit and co-op housing developers are stuck without the support required to get building new affordable homes, as Mayor Chow continues to focus on her bureaucratic public builder model.

“Half this city rents their home. NDP Mayor Chow decided that holding a press conference and passing a by-law was good enough, without doing the follow-up to make sure it worked. That is how Toronto got stuck,” said Brad Bradford, candidate for Mayor of Toronto. “Renters don’t need another announcement. They need someone to enforce the rules already on the books, make sure nobody is boiling in their own apartment in July, and get more homes built by people who aren’t going to flip the building in five years. My plan is focused around these pillars: Keep your home. Cool your home. Build more homes.”

Bradford’s plan for renters

Keep your home

-
Enforce the renoviction by-law the City already has. Bradford will increase transparency with a public dashboard, updated every quarter from Toronto Building: applications, licences issued and refused, investigations, charges, convictions, fines actually collected, and tenant compensation owed and paid. Published by address, with tenant privacy protected.

-
A costed staffing plan for Toronto Building so that when a tenant complains, somebody shows up. A rule nobody enforces is not protection.

-
Expand the eviction prevention programs that work and ensure they are appropriately funded. In 2025, Eviction Prevention in the Community (EPIC) kept 1,105 households in their homes and the Toronto Rent Bank approved 2,961 grants.

-
Ask Queen’s Park to toughen the evidence rules and penalties for bad-faith renovations and own-use evictions, and to resource the Landlord and Tenant Board so tenants get decisions in weeks, not months.

Cool your home

-
A maximum heat standard renters can count on: at least one cooled living space in every rental home. Modelled on New Westminster, B.C., where that room may not average more than 26 degrees overnight from April through October, and adapted for Toronto through public-health and legal review.

-
Delivered for Council approval by June 2027, the deadline Council already set, with a funded, phased implementation plan attached. Not another study. The phased plan will prioritize the oldest towers and Toronto Community Housing first.

-
Ask the Province to prohibit above-guideline rent increases for mandated cooling, and attach no-pass-through conditions to any cooling work the City helps pay for.

-
Expand the Air Conditioner Benefit for low-income seniors and people with medical need, and publish applications, approvals and units delivered every year so the program is measured by who got help, not who applied.

Build more homes

-
A predictable Non-Profit Density Framework so mission-driven builders get clear eligibility, standard terms and a financially tested approach to density, instead of a one-off negotiation for every site.

-
Expand the City’s revolving pre-development fund, which today offers about $10 million in interest-free loans of up to $50,000 per affordable home, with transparent terms and annual reporting. The money gets repaid and lent again. Non-profits have a pipeline of thousands of homes waiting on exactly this.

-
Publicly report the City’s housing progress more regularly and transparently, and change the main metric of success to keys in doors, not approvals on paper.

“Non-profit and co-op builders are the people who will still own the building in 40 years. They are not trying to flip it. And City Hall makes them beg, site by site, for density it should be offering as a matter of policy,” said Bradford. “Give them the rules, give them the early money, and count every home. That is how you build housing people can afford and keep.”

Today’s commitments build on two pieces of Bradford’s fiscal plan that already put money in renters’ pockets.

-
The water bill cut reaches renters. Bradford’s 25% water bill cut, about $300 a year for the average household, is applied to every resident’s bills, including Toronto Community Housing and Toronto Seniors Housing. For tenants whose landlord pays the water bill, Bradford has already written to Minister Flack asking the Province to extend section 131 of the Residential Tenancies Act, which forces a rent reduction when a landlord’s property taxes go down, to permanent municipal water rate cuts. Landlord’s water bill goes down, the rent goes down. No cost to the Province, no cost to landlords.

-
The biggest opportunity ever to buy a first home in Toronto. The City’s first-time buyer rebate was written in 2008 and today fully covers only 2.6% of homes. Bradford will eliminate the Municipal Land Transfer Tax on the value of an average home, saving people $18,000. It is the largest expansion of the first-time buyer break in the city’s history, and it is aimed at the renter trying to get over the line.

The province has never sent the City more money. NDP Mayor Chow still raised property taxes nearly 20%, starting with 9.5%, the largest increase in the city’s history. If she’s re-elected she’ll raise them another 20% at least. Food bank visits are up 340% since 2019, 4.1 million last year alone. And last month, Olivia Chow stood on the debate stage and declared that Toronto has reached its full potential.

“Tell that to the renter who got an N13 in the mail, or the senior who spent August in a 32-degree apartment waiting on an air conditioner that never came,” said Bradford. “The Mayor passed by-laws and called it progress. I’m going to deliver them with more transparency and accountability than we’ve seen in years. And I’m going to make sure a renter in this city gets the water bill cut, gets a real shot at a first home, and gets a City Hall that keeps its word. Toronto isn’t broken. It’s stuck. We’re on the cusp of change. Change is within our grasp. To change the city, we need to change the mayor.”

Background

-
Renters: 557,970 renter households, 48% of Toronto’s 1.16 million households; 223,190 of them (40%) spend 30% or more of before-tax income on shelter (City of Toronto Housing Data Book, March 2023, 2021 Census).

-
Renoviction by-law: Municipal Code Chapter 662, in force July 31, 2025, administered by Toronto Building; 2026 licence fee $728 per unit. Council requested an implementation status update by July 31, 2027. No consolidated public reporting of applications, refusals, investigations, charges or fines exists today.

-
Heat: 93% of 3,582 RentSafeTO buildings reported no building-provided air conditioning in 2024, about 271,000 units (City staff report, November 25, 2025). Council directed a maximum-temperature by-law and implementation plan for approval by June 2027 (2026.CC42.12). The June 23, 2026 staff report notes 90% of participating landlords indicated they would seek to pass compliance costs to tenants.

-
Air conditioner program: 2025 pilot delivered 489 units against 1,414 applications. For 2026 Council authorized $1.06 million in one-time reserve support on top of $1.0 million, for up to 2,400 units (2026.EX31.2).

-
New Westminster: one qualifying living space may not average more than 26°C between 8 p.m. and 8 a.m., April 1 to October 31, with specified exemptions; adopted June 2026.

-
Eviction prevention: EPIC prevented 1,105 household evictions in 2025; Toronto Rent Bank approved 2,961 grants (Housing Secretariat, March 23, 2026).

-
Non-profit finance: 2026 Community Housing Pre-development Fund offers about $10 million in interest-free loans of up to $50,000 per Affordable Rental Home. WoodGreen reported a 3,000-unit non-profit pipeline to Council in July 2024.

-
Housing targets: HousingTO target of 65,000 rent-controlled homes by 2030 (6,500 rent-geared-to-income, 41,000 affordable rental, 17,500 rent-controlled market). Council requested $557.5 million from Build Canada Homes for about 4,000 homes; the August 5, 2026 announcement identified $310 million for 9 City-land projects and more than 1,800 homes.

-
Water bill cut: 25% off the first $1,500 of every water bill, about $300 a year for the average household; applied to every account including Toronto Community Housing and Toronto Seniors Housing. Letter to Minister Flack (September 2026) requests that section 131 of the Residential Tenancies Act, which requires rent reductions when municipal property taxes on a rental building are reduced, be extended to permanent municipal water rate reductions.

-
Land Transfer Tax: municipal LTT eliminated on the value of a home below the average Toronto price ($1.1 million) for buyers who will live in the home; about $18,000 at the threshold. The existing first-time buyer rebate was set at $400,000 in 2008 and fully covers 2.6% of homes today; the Bradford plan covers up to two thirds. Investors, corporations and the value above the line continue to pay.

-
Property taxes under NDP Mayor Chow: up nearly 20% in 3 years, starting with 9.5%, the largest increase in the city’s history. Food bank visits: 4.1 million last year, up 340% since 2019 (Daily Bread Food Bank, Who’s Hungry).
