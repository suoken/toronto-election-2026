Title: How Brad Bradford Pays for His Platform: Toronto 2026 Fiscal Plan Explained
Published: 2026-07-11
Issue category: Toronto Mayoral Campaign 2026

Last updated Oct 20, 2026

Summary

Brad Bradford, candidate for Mayor of Toronto in the October 26, 2026 municipal election, has published a cost and a funding source for each of his five major commitments. Only one of the five, hiring 1,000 front-line police officers, is new ongoing spending. It phases in over four years and reaches about $143 million a year at full strength.

The other four are funded from money the City of Toronto already collects or already budgets. The one-year property tax freeze (about $100 million) is paid for from recurring operating underspend. The municipal land transfer tax exemption (about $300 million a year) is paid for by ending the practice of pre-paying capital projects out of operating revenue, plus a water utility dividend. The 25% water bill cut ends an overcharge of roughly $400 million a year. The TTC money-back guarantee is estimated at $3.5 million to $7 million a year.

The plan does not use borrowing for operating costs. The operating budget balances every year, as Ontario's Municipal Act requires. Borrowing is limited to capital assets, under two new caps on debt costs.

All figures come from City of Toronto budget documents, the City's year-end variance reports, the 2024 Financial Information Return and the Toronto Police Service 2026 budget. Sources are listed at the end of this page.

Key figures at a glance

-
One-year property tax freeze: about $100 million, one time. Funded from the City's recurring operating underspend (about $100 million in 2025, about $130 million in 2024).

-
Municipal land transfer tax exemption: about $300 million a year in forgone revenue. Funded from operating-to-capital transfers already in the City's 2026 budget ($256 million in 2027, $369 million in 2028), plus a water utility dividend (about $45 million in 2027, building toward about $125 million).

-
25% water bill cut: not a cost to taxpayers. In 2024 the water system cost $909 million to run and billed $1.32 billion.

-
TTC money-back guarantee: about $3.5 million to $7 million a year, or 0.3% to 0.7% of fare revenue.

-
1,000 front-line police officers: about $36 million in year one, rising to about $143 million a year by year four.

-
New debt rules: interest never above 4 cents per dollar of City revenue, and total debt costs never above 10% of own-source revenue. No borrowing for operating costs.

How does Brad Bradford pay for the property tax freeze?

The one-year freeze on the base City tax levy costs about $100 million, and it is paid for from money the City budgets each year but does not spend. A 2% increase, which covers normal inflation, raises roughly $100 million. That is the cost of not raising it.

The City's own year-end Operating Variance Reports show this underspend recurs. In 2025, on the property-tax-supported part of the operating budget, the City budgeted about $5.64 billion and spent about $5.54 billion, leaving close to $100 million. In 2024 the figure was about $130 million.

The underspend is structural. The City budgets every approved position at full cost whether or not it is filled, then sets property taxes as if every dollar will be spent. Mayor Olivia Chow's 2026 budget relies on the same money: it balances with $323 million of budget bridging from reserves and the 2025 surplus.

The freeze covers homes and businesses for one year, while an independent line-by-line review of City spending takes place. After that year, Brad Bradford has committed to keep increases below inflation. The freeze applies to the base City levy, not the separate City Building Fund levy, which has risen 1.5% a year since 2020 and supports long-term infrastructure financing.

How does Brad Bradford pay for cutting Toronto's land transfer tax?

The exemption costs about $300 million a year in forgone revenue, and both funding sources appear in the City's own 2026 Budget Summary. Toronto collects about $850 million a year from its municipal land transfer tax, forecast to rise to $923 million and then $988 million.

Who qualifies: first-time buyers, and Toronto homeowners selling one principal residence to buy another. The tax is eliminated on the value of the home below the previous year's average Toronto resale price, which was $1.1 million for 2025. Value above that threshold is still taxed. The December 2025 rates on homes over $3 million stay in place. Investors and landlords with more than one property (29% of purchases), corporations and commercial buyers continue to pay in full.

What it saves: on a $1.1 million home, the municipal land transfer tax is about $18,000. The current first-time buyer rebate has been fixed at the tax on a $400,000 home since 2008 and fully exempts about 2.6% of homes. The new threshold covers up to two thirds of resale homes.

Funding source one is the operating revenue the City already plans to move into the capital budget. The 2026 Budget Summary earmarks $130 million of land transfer tax revenue in 2027 and $195 million in 2028 for capital (page 265), plus $126 million in 2027 and $174 million in 2028 of general revenue as "capital from current" (page 263). That totals about $256 million in 2027 and $369 million in 2028. The plan stops these transfers and finances the same capital over the life of each asset instead, as the federal government, every province and Toronto Hydro already do.

Funding source two is a dividend from a publicly owned water utility: about $45 million in 2027 while the utility is set up, building toward about $125 million at maturity. For comparison, Winnipeg's water utility pays its city a dividend of 10% of revenue.

In 2027 the transfers plus the dividend cover the $300 million. From 2028 the transfers alone exceed it. The dividend is a backstop if land transfer tax revenue falls in a weak housing market. No project in the capital plan is cut; what changes is when and how it is paid for.

Why is the 25% water bill cut not a cost to taxpayers?

The water bill cut is not new spending; it ends an overcharge. In 2024 Toronto's water and wastewater system cost $909 million to run, including amortization, and billed users $1.32 billion. That is roughly $400 million a year collected beyond the cost of running the system, and 2025 was similar.

The cut is about $300 a year for the average household, whose 2026 bill is $1,118. Today 67 cents of every water dollar goes to capital rather than delivering water. The water capital reserve has grown from $141 million in 2009 to about $2 billion, losing purchasing power to inflation. Toronto Water carries no debt and underspent its 2025 budget by nearly $400 million.

Under the plan, water becomes a City-owned utility run like Toronto Hydro. It borrows against pipes and plants that last 50 to 100 years and recovers the cost over their life, instead of collecting the cash up front. Savings are capped for bills above $1,500 a year, so the cut is focused on residents rather than large commercial, institutional and industrial users.

Ownership stays 100% public, protected three ways: Brad Bradford's commitment, a City bylaw permanently barring private investment, and Ontario's Water and Wastewater Public Corporations Act, 2025, as amended by Bill 98 in May 2026, which restricts shares to a municipality, the Province, the Government of Canada or their agents. Edmonton, Windsor, Kingston and Halifax already run water this way, and Peel Region is moving to it.

What does the TTC money-back guarantee cost?

The guarantee is estimated to cost $3.5 million to $7 million a year, or 0.3% to 0.7% of TTC fare revenue. Riders whose trip is delayed 15 minutes or more get a refund or credit. The TTC designs the details, with exceptions for events outside its control such as extreme weather.

The estimate is based on experience elsewhere. GO Transit has offered a service guarantee since 2012, and London, Singapore and Washington, D.C. run similar programs. On Washington's WMATA system, about 0.3% of trips received a refund. The TTC carried about 420 million trips in 2024 and collected roughly $1.0 billion in fares. If about 0.5% of trips receive a credit, the cost falls in the range above. If claims run higher, caps can be tightened.

No new fare gates are needed. Riders submit their trip details, and a refund is issued when the claim matches TTC operating records. The TTC controls the cost by running on time, which makes the guarantee an accountability tool rather than a significant budget line.

What would 1,000 new police officers cost?

Hiring 1,000 front-line officers would cost about $143 million a year at full strength, reached in year four. This is the one commitment that adds new ongoing spending, and Brad Bradford has said so since he announced it.

The arithmetic uses the City's own numbers. The Toronto Police Service 2026 budget allocates $20.5 million to hire 143 new officers, about $143,000 per officer fully loaded. Hiring roughly 250 officers a year costs about $36 million in year one, rising to about $143 million in year four. Costs grow as recruits move up the constable pay grid, which is why hiring is phased over a full term. Councillor Shelley Carroll's estimate of "well over $100 million" is consistent with this figure.

For scale, the police budget is about $1.43 billion and rose about $94 million, roughly 7%, in 2026 alone. That increase came in a year when Mayor Chow's budget held the operating property tax increase to 0.7%. At full strength the commitment equals about 10% of today's police budget, reached over four years, and can be managed through the annual budget process.

Why 1,000: Toronto's population has grown 21% since 2010, about half a million people, with about the same number of deployed officers. The Police Services Board's hiring plan, approved December 12, 2024, takes deployed strength from 5,433 to 5,935 by 2029, or 172 officers per 100,000 residents. Montreal has 221, Vancouver 196, Edmonton 188 and Winnipeg 186. The current plan adds only about 250 officers during the next council term; the 1,000 officers would be on patrol, in neighbourhoods and at subway stations.

Does Brad Bradford's platform cost $6 billion?

No. The $6 billion figure was produced by Mayor Olivia Chow's campaign on October 1, 2026, without published calculations. Adding the costs above gives a total far below it: about $100 million once for the freeze, about $300 million a year for the land transfer tax, $3.5 million to $7 million a year for the TTC guarantee, and up to $143 million a year for police.

The only way to approach $6 billion is to count capital projects, such as platform edge doors, as new operating spending. These are long-lived assets financed over their life, the same way Toronto Hydro and every Canadian province finance infrastructure.

What happens if the line-by-line review finds less than expected?

Nothing in the plan depends on the review finding a specific number. Each commitment has its own funding source, listed above, and Brad Bradford has not promised a savings target from the review.

The review's purpose is to look for waste, duplication and programs that have outlived their purpose, not to cut front-line services. Toronto's operating budget was about $9 billion at the last comprehensive review in 2011 and is over $18 billion today. Examples of the waste it would target include $11 million the Auditor General found spent on software licences for people who no longer work for the City.

Brad Bradford: "I'm a city planner by training. When something isn't working, you don't guess. You look under the hood. We're on the cusp of change in this city, and change starts with knowing where the money went."

How have Toronto's finances changed since 2023?

Toronto's operating budget rose from $16.16 billion in 2023 to $18.9 billion in 2026, an increase of $2.7 billion or about 17% in three years. The 10-year capital plan is a record $63.1 billion.

Residential property taxes rose 9.5% in 2024, 6.9% in 2025 and 2.2% in 2026, nearly 20% compounded, or almost $700 a year on the average home. The 2024 increase was the largest in the city's history.

The spending growth has not closed the City's structural gap. The 2024 budget opened with about $1.8 billion in pressures, and the City's 2026 Budget Summary projects a $1.6 billion gap for 2027 (page 14). This came alongside new provincial support under the November 2023 Ontario-Toronto New Deal, including the upload of the Gardiner Expressway and Don Valley Parkway.

Over the same period, food bank visits in Toronto reached 4.1 million, up 340% since 2019, according to Daily Bread Food Bank and North York Harvest.

Frequently asked questions

Is Brad Bradford's platform costed? Yes. Each commitment was published with a cost and funding source when it was announced, using City of Toronto budget documents.

Which commitment adds new spending? Only the 1,000 police officers, at about $143 million a year by year four.

Is the property tax freeze permanent? No. It is a one-year freeze on the base City levy. After that, increases are committed to stay below inflation.

Does the freeze include the City Building Fund levy? No. The City Building Fund levy is separate and supports long-term infrastructure financing.

Is the underspend that funds the freeze one-time money? No. The City underspent its tax-supported operating budget by about $130 million in 2024 and about $100 million in 2025, and the current budget itself relies on prior-year surplus.

Will the water utility be privatized? No. Provincial law as amended in May 2026 restricts shares to public governments, and a City bylaw would permanently bar private investment.

Will the City borrow to pay operating costs? No. Borrowing is limited to capital, and the operating budget must balance every year under the Municipal Act.

What if land transfer tax revenue drops? The water utility dividend is a backstop, and from 2028 the operating-to-capital transfers alone exceed the $300 million cost.

Does the plan depend on savings from the line-by-line review? No. None of the commitments is funded by the review.
