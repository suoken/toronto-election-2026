Title: BRADFORD CALLS FOR MANDATORY ADDICTION TREATMENT NOW: 'WE DON'T NEED ANOTHER REVIEW'
Published: 2026-08-17
Issue category: Toronto Mayoral Campaign 2026

Mandatory treatment helps the person, helps first responders, helps our communities. Mayor Chow is slowing down a compassionate response.

TORONTO, ON | Brad Bradford today called for mandatory addiction treatment now, saying Toronto can't spend two more years stepping around people dying in its parks, on its streets and on its transit while help sits behind a 72-day wait. Bradford's call comes a day after Ontario's Big City Mayors asked the province, for the second time in two years, to review the Mental Health Act and the Health Care Consent Act, more or less, the same review the caucus requested in October 2024.

"Toronto needs mandatory addiction treatment for the most severe cases, and we need it now. Not after another study, not after another review, not after two more years of letters," said Bradford. "I appreciate that twenty-nine leaders must agree on every word in a communique, and a stern call for review is commendable. But to be clear, we don’t need a review to determine if this is a problem. We need reform to stop it from being one. We need beds, we need change to the law, and we need the will to act."

"I'm a father. I can't step around an unconscious human being on the sidewalk, holding my daughters' hands, and teach them that's normal," said Bradford. "Some people look at our streets and talk about zombies. I won't. Those are people, somebody's kid, somebody's parent, and they need our help. It's time to bring order and compassion back to this city. We can have both. We must have both."

Two years into the review era, the crisis has deepened. Toronto paramedics responded to 350 suspected non-fatal overdose calls in January 2026, up 46 per cent from a year earlier, and April 2026 was the worst month since 2021. Ontarians seeking intensive residential treatment still wait an average of 16 days for an assessment and 72 days for admission.

"Other mayors have stopped waiting. Ken Sim, the mayor of Vancouver, is putting involuntary care beds to his residents this fall, and he told his own premier: 'We don't need another study. We don't need another plan. We need meaningful action now,'" said Bradford. "He's right. And I agree with Wab Kinew, the NDP Premier of Manitoba, when he said, 'People suffering from meth addiction do not have the right to determine how the rest of us are going to live in our society.' A mayor in Vancouver. A premier in Manitoba. This isn't left versus right. It's leadership versus looking away."

"And where has Toronto been? For two years, the Mayor's answer has been letters asking for funding. To this day, I haven’t heard Olivia Chow say the word: mandatory," said Bradford. "Toronto doesn't need a consensus position. It needs a plan."

Bradford's plan, announced Friday, commits that as Mayor he will:

• Call on the province to introduce a mandatory treatment law covering adults whose severe addiction has stripped their capacity to make treatment decisions and who face a serious, near-term risk of harm, with the same independent review Ontario already provides for severe mental illness.

• Fight for Toronto's full share of new residential treatment, withdrawal management and supportive housing beds, so treatment is there when people ask for it.

• Commit to at least 100 recovery house beds in Toronto: structured, live-in recovery homes where people can stay for up to a year, modelled on the recovery communities already operating in Alberta, fast-tracked through a new zoning class for accredited treatment and recovery homes and supported by funding from the provincial government and not-for-profit partners.

• Meet the crisis where it shows up by expanding co-response teams that pair police officers with mental health and addiction clinicians and embedding outreach workers on the TTC and in community with direct referral pathways into treatment.

• Hold Queen's Park to results: a commitment to continuous addiction medication through treatment and release, and independent, published evaluation written into the law from day one.

On the mayors' call for an emergency declaration, Bradford said he will support anything that gets beds built faster: "But you can't declare your way out of a seventy-two day wait. Declarations don't treat anyone. Access to critical care does."

"When addiction gets so severe that someone no longer has the capacity to make important life decisions on their own, and they are at risk of losing their life, the rest of us have a collective responsibility to help by making compassionate life-saving decisions for them, and getting them the help they need," said Bradford. "That's how we help people get better, and that's how we give parks back to families and make our city feel safe again."
