Title: BRADFORD UNVEILS PLAN TO CLEAN UP, MAKE SAFE AND RENAME YONGE AND DUNDAS AS TORONTO SQUARE
Published: 2026-06-29
Issue category: Clean City

A plan to restore the country’s busiest public square, honour the Canadians who built this country, and ask the property taxpayer for nothing.

TORONTO, ON. Mayoral candidate Brad Bradford today released a plan to restore the public square at Yonge and Dundas, currently named Sankofa Square, as a clean, safe and vibrant heart of the city, and to rename it Toronto Square. The plan would be delivered through a partnership with an iconic Canadian brand, at no cost to the property taxpayer.

The square sits on the busiest intersection in the country, where more than 100,000 people cross every day. Under the plan, the City would set published cleaning and safety standards for the space, look to establish a permanent police substation staffed by officers already on the force, expand outreach and mental health support for people in crisis, and fill the square with a year-round calendar of commerce, culture, food, retail and live performance.

“This is the first thing visitors see of our city, and right now it tells them we have given up,” said Bradford. “It is dirty, it is unsafe, and families walk a little faster to get through it. That is not a tragedy. It is a choice, and I am making a different one.”

Between events and advertising, the square’s screens would carry a rotating tribute to the Canadians who built this country and shaped the world, among them Viola Desmond, Terry Fox, Tom Longboat, Sir Frederick Banting, and Lucie and Thornton Blackburn.

“A great public square should remind us who we are,” said Bradford. “Canadians from every community that has made this city home, honoured by name, in lights, in the heart of downtown, every single day.”

Honourees would be chosen by an independent, broadly representative panel rather than by the Mayor’s office. The renaming would require a vote of City Council, which Bradford committed to bringing forward and championing.

The plan carries no cost to the property taxpayer. The cleanup, the programming and the renaming would be financed through a partnership with an iconic Canadian brand, supported by the advertisers and businesses that profit from the corner. It mirrors the approach the City itself adopted at the last renaming, when any cost above its own budget was to be carried by private partners and in-kind support. The officers who staff the new substation would be drawn from existing Toronto Police Service resources and directed to the busiest public space in the city.

“Not one dollar of this lands on your property tax bill,” said Bradford. “The people who profit from that corner can help us restore it. I want a city we can be proud of again. Not someday. Now.”

BACKGROUNDER

Toronto Square

The square today

The public square at Yonge and Dundas is the busiest gathering place in the country. It is ringed by the Eaton Centre, the Ed Mirvish Theatre and Massey Hall, and it sits above a major transit hub. For years the space has struggled with cleanliness and safety, including visible drug use, and with a growing sense among residents and visitors that it has been allowed to decline. The plan treats that decline as fixable, not permanent.

The plan, in three parts

1. Clean and safe. Published cleaning and maintenance standards, so the public bar is clear and the City has to meet it. A steady safety presence, including looking to establish a permanent police substation staffed by officers already on the force. Expanded outreach, mental health support and housing for people in crisis in and around the square.

2. Alive again. A year-round programming calendar of commerce, culture, food, retail and live performance, developed with the surrounding businesses and the Business Improvement Area to draw families back.

3. A tribute to our story. The square’s permanent digital screens would carry a rotating tribute to celebrated Canadians between events and advertising.

Funding

The plan carries no cost to the property taxpayer. The cleanup, the programming and the renaming would be financed through a partnership with an iconic Canadian brand, supported by the advertisers and businesses that operate around the square. This mirrors the funding approach the City adopted during the previous renaming, when costs above its own budget were to be covered by third-party financial partners and in-kind support. The officers who staff the new police substation would be deployed from existing Toronto Police Service resources, directed to the busiest public space in the city.

Canadian icons to honour (illustrative, to be finalized by an independent panel)

• Viola Desmond: refused to leave a whites-only section of a Nova Scotia cinema in 1946, and now appears on the ten dollar bill.

• Terry Fox: his 1980 Marathon of Hope made him the country’s most enduring symbol of courage and a lasting inspiration for cancer research.

• Tom Longboat: Onondaga distance runner from Six Nations, winner of the 1907 Boston Marathon and a Toronto sporting legend.

• Sir Frederick Banting: co-discoverer of insulin at the University of Toronto and a Nobel laureate whose work has saved millions of lives.

• Lucie and Thornton Blackburn: owned Toronto’s first cab company, built homes for newcomers and were community activists who lived near today’s Distillery District.

Process and governance

• The square is owned by the City of Toronto and operated by a Board of Management. Renaming requires a vote of City Council. Bradford would bring the motion forward and make the case.

• Honourees would be chosen by an independent, broadly representative panel, not by the Mayor’s office.

• The square is currently named Sankofa Square. It was renamed from Yonge-Dundas Square by City Council in December 2023 and launched under the new name in August 2025.

Quick facts

• More than 100,000 people cross the intersection each day, the busiest in the country.

• The square is roughly one acre, opened in 2002, and is operated through the first public-private partnership of its kind for a public square in Canada.

• It is often compared to Times Square in New York for its large video screens. New York has operated a police substation in the heart of Times Square for decades, and its most recent renovation was funded through a public-private partnership led by the New York City Police Foundation.
