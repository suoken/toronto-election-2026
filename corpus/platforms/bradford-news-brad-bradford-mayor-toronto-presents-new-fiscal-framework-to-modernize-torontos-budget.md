Title: Bradford presents new fiscal framework to modernize Toronto's budget
Published: 2026-10-01
Issue category: Cost of Living

TORONTO, ON | Brad Bradford today set out how a Bradford administration will modernize the way Toronto budgets, the way the federal government did 30 years ago and the Ontario government did 20 years ago. Toronto has the eighth-largest government budget in Canada, larger than four provinces, and still keeps its books the way it did in the 1990s.

City Hall says its operating and capital budgets are separate. On paper, they are. In practice, hundreds of millions of dollars a year are moved out of the operating budget, funded by that year’s property taxes and land transfer tax, to pre-pay for capital projects that taxpayers won’t use for years. The City’s own 2026 Budget already earmarks about $256 million of those transfers for 2027 and about $369 million for 2028. Ending this cross-subsidy will allow Bradford to deliver on his transformational commitments to eliminate the Municipal Land Transfer Tax for the average homebuyer while maintaining capital investments, with no service cuts. Last year, more than $1 billion of the capital budget went unspent, meaning important projects the city committed to building were not delivered. That is one reason why property taxes go up every year for infrastructure that residents don’t get to use and the pipes, community centres and transit stations still wait.

“Everyone at City Hall agrees Toronto has a structural budget problem. NDP Mayor Chow was elected to fix it. She started with a $1.7 billion hole, left over from the pandemic. Three years and nearly 20% in tax increases later, she is leaving a $1.6 billion hole for next year. She didn’t fix the problem. She just sent you the bill,” said Brad Bradford, candidate for Mayor of Toronto. “The problem was never that Toronto is poor. The problem is a budget rulebook from the 1990s that overtaxes people today to pay cash for things that don’t get built. Residents are done with Mayor Chow overtaxing, misspending, and underbuilding. I’m going to change the rulebook and modernize the budget.”

For eight years, through a pandemic that blew a hole in every big-city budget in the country, Toronto kept property tax increases at or below inflation. It took discipline every single year, because the rulebook never made it easy. In 2023 the discipline left the building. "Holding the line shouldn't depend on who's sitting in the chair," said Bradford.

"Our Prime Minister has modernized our country’s books, with separate operating and capital budgets. Truly embracing the same practices here gives Toronto a modern, sustainable budget that protects services today and lets us invest more in transit, roads and community centres."

Bradford’s plan to modernize the budget

-
A real separation of the operating and capital budgets. The operating budget, which runs the city, is paid for by taxes and fees and balances every year. The capital budget, which builds the city, is financed over the life of each asset, the way a family pays for a home.

-
End the cross subsidy. No more shipping hundreds of millions of dollars a year from the operating budget to pre-pay the capital budget, which Mayor Chow doesn’t actually deliver on. That money goes back to the people who paid it by eliminating the double land transfer tax on the value of a principal home below the average Toronto price.

-
The tightest debt rules in the country. Debt interest never above 4 cents of every revenue dollar, lower than any province, Chicago, New York or Montreal. Total debt costs never above 10% of own-source revenue. Borrowing for building in the capital budget only, never for operating expenses.

-
A year one property tax freeze. The first full, independent, line by line review of City spending since 2011, before anyone at City Hall asks taxpayers for another dollar. After the first year, property taxes grow below inflation. Frontline service cuts are off the table.

-
A better way to build and fund infrastructure. Reduce water bills by $300 a year for residents. Establish Toronto Water as a 100% municipally-owned utility and find savings from the 67% of the budget that currently goes towards capital reserves.

Nothing here is untested. Ottawa modernized its books in the 1990s to stop pre-financing infrastructure and in its most recent budget drew a clear line between what it spends to run the country and what it invests to build it. Queen’s Park made the same change two decades ago. Toronto Hydro has financed 100-year assets this way since 1999. Toronto is the outlier among major governments.

The choice on October 26

The province has never sent the City more money. NDP Mayor Chow still raised property taxes nearly 20%, starting with 9.5%, the largest increase in the city’s history. If she’s re-elected, she’ll raise them another 20% at least. Food bank visits are up 340% since 2019, 4.1 million last year alone. Olivia Chow stood on the debate stage and declared that Toronto has reached its full potential.

“That’s the choice. The status quo, another 20% on your property taxes, and a hole in the budget that never gets smaller. Or we do it a different way, the way every other government in this country the size of Toronto already does it, and break the cycle of overtaxing and underbuilding,” said Bradford. “Toronto isn’t broken. It’s stuck. We’re on the cusp of change. Change is within our grasp. To change the city, we need to change the mayor.”

Background

-
Operating to capital transfers already planned in the City’s 2026 Budget: $130 million of land transfer tax revenue in 2027 and $195 million in 2028 (2026 Budget, p. 265), plus $126 million in 2027 and $174 million in 2028 of general revenue (“capital from current”, 2026 Budget, p. 263). Combined: about $256 million in 2027 and $369 million in 2028.

-
More than $1 billion of the 2025 capital budget went unspent (Capital Variance Report, 12 months ended December 31, 2025). The 2026 Budget plans lower capital spending than 2025 (2026 Budget Summary, p. 272).

-
Toronto Water: 67 cents of every water dollar goes to a capital reserve. Reserves have grown from $141 million in 2009 to about $2 billion. The system cost $909 million to run in 2024 and billed $1.32 billion.

-
Debt rules under the Bradford plan: debt interest capped at 4 cents per revenue dollar; total debt costs capped at 10% of own-source revenue; operating budget balanced every year as the Municipal Act requires.

-
Property taxes under NDP Mayor Chow: up nearly 20% in 3 years, starting with 9.5%, the largest increase in the city’s history. Food bank visits: 4.1 million last year, up 340% since 2019 (Daily Bread Food Bank, Who’s Hungry).
