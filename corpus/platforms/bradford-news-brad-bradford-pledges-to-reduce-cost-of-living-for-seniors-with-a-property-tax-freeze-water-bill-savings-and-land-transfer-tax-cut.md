Title: BRAD BRADFORD PLEDGES TO REDUCE COST OF LIVING FOR SENIORS WITH A PROPERTY TAX FREEZE, WATER BILL SAVINGS AND LAND TRANSFER TAX CUT
Published: 2026-10-15
Issue category: Cost of Living

TORONTO, ON | This morning in North York, Brad Bradford sat down with a group of seniors who share the same story. They raised their families here. They built community and gave back. And at the end of every month, they look at their stack of bills and hope they’ll be able to pay them all.

The seniors at the table told Bradford what he hears at doors across the city: the bills keep climbing and the city keeps working worse. Bradford shared his plan to lower their costs, starting with freezing property taxes in year one, reducing water bills by up to 25%, saving about $300 a year for the average household, and eliminating Toronto’s double land transfer tax on the homes most people buy, all backed by the first full, independent, line by line review of City spending since 2011.

"These are the people who built this city. The very least they should be able to expect is to grow old in it, in the community they built, with dignity and support," said Bradford. "Instead, NDP Mayor Olivia Chow raised their property taxes by nearly 20%, including 9.5%, the largest increase in the city’s history. But their services haven’t gotten any better. That’s why as Mayor, I will give them a break with a property tax freeze in my first year.”

Some of the seniors paid off their homes over 30 or 40 years. The kids have moved out, the house is too big, the stairs are getting harder, and they're ready for something smaller and more accessible. They want to stay in the neighbourhood, near their church, their doctor, their friends and their grandchildren. But the city’s taxes encourage them to stay put.

Toronto is the only city in Canada that charges its own land transfer tax on top of the province's. For someone selling the family home to buy a smaller one, that means paying the land transfer tax twice, to the province and to the City, on a purchase they're making so they can stay in Toronto. The City offers no break at all for people who already own a home here. The only break it offers, the first-time buyer rebate, was set at $400,000 in 2008 and hasn't been touched since. The only change NDP Mayor Chow has made to the land transfer tax is to raise it.

“Seniors deserve more choice over how and where they live. But the moment they try to move from the family home to a condo down the street, City Hall hands them a five figure bill in cash on closing day,” said Bradford. So they stay in a house that no longer fits, and a young family that needs that house never gets the chance. That's not a market problem. That's a City Hall problem."

As mayor, Bradford will eliminate Toronto's municipal land transfer tax on the value of a principal residence below the average Toronto home price, $1.1 million in 2025, with the threshold moving with the market every year. A new Principal Residence Rebate extends that break to Toronto homeowners who sell one home to buy another in the city. A couple downsizing from the family home to an $800,000 condo in North York would pay no municipal land transfer tax at all, about $12,500 back on closing day. On a home at the $1.1 million threshold, it's about $18,000. Up to two thirds of the homes sold in Toronto would change hands with no city land transfer tax. Investors, landlords, corporations and homes above $3 million keep paying. It's funded with money the City already collects, and not one service is cut.

"A city that taxes people for moving is a city that stops moving," said Bradford. "When a senior can afford to downsize, a young family gets a house with a backyard and a school down the street, and the senior gets to stay close to everything she knows. Today the tax punishes both of them. I'm going to end it on the homes most people in this city actually buy."

The seniors at the table told Bradford what he hears at doors across North York: the bills keep climbing and the city keeps working worse. The province has never given the City of Toronto more money than it has over the past 3 years. NDP Mayor Chow still raised property taxes nearly 20%, the largest increase in the city's history. If she's re-elected she'll raise them another 20% at least. For someone on a fixed income, that increase is the difference between staying in your home and selling it. In those same 3 years, food bank visits climbed to 4.1 million a year, up 340% since 2019. Where did the money go?

Safety came up at every turn. People who have lived on the same street for 40 years described not feeling safe walking to the plaza after dark, not taking the subway downtown anymore, and seeing break-ins on the news in neighbourhoods that never used to make the news. As mayor, Bradford will take his seat on the Toronto Police Services Board, put 1,000 more front line officers on the street with a visible police presence at every subway station, go after the repeat violent offenders terrorizing the same neighbourhoods over and over, and bring in mandatory treatment for people whose addiction has taken over their lives.

"A city that is not safe is not free," said Bradford. "Someone who has lived on the same street for 40 years should not have to think twice about walking to the pharmacy."

NDP Mayor Chow stood on the debate stage and declared that Toronto has reached its full potential. Bradford rejects that. In a city where the people who built it can't afford to move down the street, and the young families who need their homes can't afford to move in, we're not even close.

"Toronto isn't broken. It's stuck. And nobody is more stuck than the people who built this city and can't afford to move within it," said Bradford. "These seniors remember a more affordable life before Olivia Chow became mayor, and they’re looking forward to a more affordable life once she’s gone.”

“We're on the cusp of change. Change is within our grasp,” said Bradford. “On October 26, the people of this city can choose a city they can afford, a city that moves, and a city that feels safe. But to change the city, we need to change the mayor."
