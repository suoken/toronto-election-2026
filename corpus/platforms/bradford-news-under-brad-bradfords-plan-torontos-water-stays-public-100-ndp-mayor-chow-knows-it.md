Title: UNDER BRADFORD’S PLAN TORONTO’S WATER STAYS PUBLIC. 100%. NDP MAYOR CHOW KNOWS IT.
Published: 2026-10-05
Issue category: Cost of Living

Brad Bradford issued the following statement in response to a news release from the Chow campaign:

“Let me say this as directly as I can. Under my plan, Toronto’s water stays 100% publicly owned. Not 99%. Not public with an asterisk. 100%, permanently. The utility will be owned by the taxpayers of Toronto, the bylaws will bar private investment of any kind, and the provincial law that creates these utilities expressly prohibits private ownership. NDP Mayor Olivia Chow knows all of that.

“Here’s what she didn’t tell you. The law she’s warning you about bans private ownership of water utilities. The Water and Wastewater Public Corporations Act says shares can only be held by a municipality, the province or the federal government. Not an investor. Not a pension fund. Not an American company. Presumably, she’s read the Act. She’s counting on you not to.

“She also didn’t tell you that Toronto already runs its most important utility exactly this way. Toronto Hydro has been a publicly owned corporation since 1999. It borrows to build, it charges what it actually costs to keep the lights on, and it pays a dividend back to the city. Nobody calls Toronto Hydro privatized. Edmonton delivers water this way. So do Windsor, Kingston and Halifax. Peel Region is doing it right now. Calgary’s independent expert panel recommended it after their water crisis. Olivia Chow is telling you that every one of those cities sold off its water. They didn’t.

“So why is she doing this? Because she can’t defend her own record on water. The average family in this city pays about $1,100 a year for water. That bill is up 75% since 2009, and it went up again every year she’s been mayor. Only about a third of it pays for the water you use. The rest is City Hall charging you today, in full, for pipes that won’t be built until 2050. Toronto Water is sitting on about $2 billion in reserves collected from residents who were told it was the price of water. Last year it underspent its own budget by nearly $400 million. That’s your money. NDP Mayor Chow would rather frighten you than give it back.

“Since 2023 the province has given this city more money than any mayor in Toronto’s history. Olivia Chow still raised property taxes nearly 20%, the largest increase in the city’s history. If she’s re-elected, she’ll raise them another 20% at least. She stood on the debate stage and declared that Toronto has reached its full potential. I don’t accept that. We’re not even close.

“My plan cuts every water bill in this city by 25%, about $300 a year for the average household, automatically, with no application and not one service cut. It keeps the water public. It keeps every Toronto Water employee doing the same job under the same contract.

“Toronto isn’t broken. It’s stuck. Stuck with a mayor who has run out of ideas and is down to making things up about mine. We’re on the cusp of change. Change is within our grasp. And to change the city, we need to change the mayor.”

BACKGROUND

Ownership. The Water and Wastewater Public Corporations Act, 2025, as amended by Bill 98 (passed May 14, 2026), provides that shares in a municipal water corporation may only be held by a municipality, the Province of Ontario, the Government of Canada or an agent of any of them. Private ownership is expressly prohibited. Core service assets cannot be divested unless declared surplus. Bradford has committed that City bylaws will additionally bar any private investment in the utility.

Employees. Existing employment contracts and collective agreements carry over to the new utility under the provincial framework.

Comparators. Toronto Hydro (City-owned since 1999, pays a dividend to the City). EPCOR, Edmonton (100% city-owned). Enwin Utilities, Windsor. Utilities Kingston. Halifax Water. Peel Region is transitioning to the model. Calgary’s independent expert review panel recommended a utility model after the 2024 Bearspaw feeder main failure.

The numbers. Average household water and wastewater bill: $1,118 a year (City of Toronto 2026 Water and Wastewater Rate Budget), up 75% since 2009, increased in each of the last 3 years. Toronto Water underspent its 2025 budget by nearly $400 million. Water and wastewater reserves have grown from $141 million in 2009 to about $2 billion.

The plan. A 25% cut on the first $1,500 of charges on every bill, about $300 a year for the average household. Rates set by an independent, skills-based board appointed by Council, within guidance set by Council, with the City as sole shareholder. Bradford has written to Minister Flack asking that the Residential Tenancies Act rent-reduction mechanism be extended to permanent municipal water rate reductions so renters benefit too.
