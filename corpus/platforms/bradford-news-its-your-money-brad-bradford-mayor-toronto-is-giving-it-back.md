Title: It's your money. Brad Bradford is giving it back
Published: 2026-09-28
Issue category: Cost of Living

Brad Bradford today announced that as mayor he will cut Toronto water bills by 25%. For the average household, that's about $300 a year back in your pocket. It happens automatically. No form to fill out, no means test, no waiting. If you pay a water bill in Toronto, when Brad Bradford is mayor, your bill goes down.

This isn't new spending. It's your money, and Bradford is giving it back, because the way Toronto pays for water means City Hall has been charging you far more than it needs to. Roughly $400 million more than the system costs to run. Toronto Water now has about $2 billion sitting in reserves, collected from residents who were told it was the price of water.

Here's how it works today. The average Toronto household pays about $1,100 a year for water. Only about a third of that pays for the water you actually use. The rest is City Hall charging you today, in full, up front, for pipes and treatment plants that will last 100 years and won't be finished for decades. It's like paying cash for your whole house on the day you move in instead of taking out a mortgage. Nobody would do that. But that's exactly how Toronto pays for water, and that's why bills are up 75% since 2009.

"You've been overpaying for years, and City Hall has been sitting on the difference. That's not a revenue problem. That's a leadership problem, it’s a management problem," said Bradford. "I'm going to fix how we pay for water, and every dollar we no longer need to take from you goes straight back to you. 25% off your bill, about $300 a year, automatically on the first $1,500 of water bills. That's real change, and it's within our grasp."

The fix is simple, and Toronto already does it for electricity. Toronto Hydro is a publicly owned utility that borrows to build assets that will last for generations, and charges you what it actually costs to deliver power. Bradford will modernize Toronto Water into a publicly owned utility that works the same way: spread the cost of big infrastructure over the decades it serves, like a mortgage, and stop billing this year's residents for the next generation's pipes. The utility will always be 100% owned by Toronto taxpayers. No private investment, ever. The bylaws will permanently bar it. Rates will be set by an independent board, not politicians, and they'll be lower than today's because the city stops double-billing you for far-off capital.

Other cities figured this out a long time ago. Edmonton, Windsor and Kingston already deliver water this way. Peel Region is moving to it now and Niagara is close behind. When Calgary's water crisis hit in 2024, the independent expert panel that looked under the hood recommended exactly this model, and Calgary is building towards it. The Ontario Government has passed the legislation to let municipalities do it. So why hasn't Toronto?

Status quo thinking. The same thinking that raised your property taxes nearly 20% in 3 years, starting with 9.5%, the largest increase in the city's history, and the looming threat that NDP Mayor Chow will raise them another 20% at least if she is re-elected.

The same thinking that took more money from the province than any mayor in Toronto's history and still couldn't tell you where it went. The same thinking that let Olivia Chow stand on the debate stage and declare that Toronto has reached its full potential. Bradford rejects that. We're not even close, and the money to do better has been coming out of your pocket the whole time.

The same status quo thinking runs through the entire City budget, which still keeps its books the way it did in the 1990s and is underinvesting in the infrastructure we need today. Last year, NDP Mayor Chow left more than $1 billion in the capital budget unspent. Bradford will modernize Toronto's budget so major infrastructure is paid for over the life of the asset, like a mortgage, under a hard cap on debt interest lower than Chicago, New York, Montreal or any province.

"Toronto isn't broken. It's stuck. Stuck paying now for pipes that won’t be built until 2050 because nobody at City Hall has bothered to ask if there's a better way. Stuck with a budget rulebook from the 1990s that nobody bothered to update," said Bradford. "Other cities asked. Toronto Hydro asked. I'm asking. And the answer is about $300 a year back to every household in this city, without cutting a single service. To change the city, we need to change the mayor. On October 26, Toronto can choose change: a city you can afford, a city that moves, a city that feels safe. A Toronto we can be proud of."

BACKGROUND: MODERNIZING THE BUDGET

The problem: Toronto's budget rules require most long-lived infrastructure to be paid for up front from current property taxes, water rates and development charges, before the asset is built. The federal government moved away from this approach in the 1990s, and so did Ontario in the 2000s. Toronto's status quo policy unnecessarily limits the city's ability to make long-term investments and does not reflect the needs or capacity of the eighth-largest government budget in Canada.

The record: in the 2025 capital budget, the City left more than $1 billion unspent (City of Toronto Capital Variance Report for the twelve months ended December 31, 2025, page 2). The 2026 budget plans to spend less on capital than 2025 (City of Toronto 2026 Budget Summary, page 272).

The plan: Toronto will pay for more major infrastructure over its useful life, modernizing its policy to account for the full range of the City's own-source revenue in calculating its fiscal capacity. The city will set a firm fiscal anchor that debt interest costs never exceed 4 cents of every dollar of revenue, lower than any province or comparable North American city, making it the most fiscally sustainable major government in North America. As required by provincial law, the operating budget balances every year.

What it buys: faster progress on the State of Good Repair backlog and new investments such as platform edge doors and community centres, without new taxes and without service cuts.

BACKGROUND: THE WATER BILL CUT

What it is: a 25% cut on the first $1,500 of every Toronto water bill, around $300 a year, for the average household bill of $1,118. Applied automatically to every bill. No application, no means test. Capping the cut at the first $1,500 means heavy users and commercial accounts don't get an ever-larger benefit.

Why it's possible: the City's own 2026 Toronto Water budget says 67% of what residents pay goes to the capital reserve contribution, not to delivering water. In 2024, according to Toronto’s Financial Information Return filed to the Ministry of Municipal Affairs, the system cost $909 million to run, including the cost of wearing out its assets, and collected $1.32 billion in user fees, roughly $400 million in over-collection in a single year. 2025 saw a similar surplus. In 2025, Toronto Water reported that it underspent its budget by nearly $400 million.

Water and wastewater reserves have grown from $141 million in 2009 to about $2 billion today. Toronto Water carries no debt. Spreading that cost over the useful life of the assets, the same way Toronto Hydro and cities like Edmonton, Kingston and Windsor already do, removes the overcharge without touching service.

How it works: Toronto Water is modernized into a publicly owned utility, always 100% owned by Toronto taxpayers, with private investment permanently barred by bylaw. Rates are set by an independent board, not politicians. Provincial enabling legislation exists and the model is currently being set up in Peel Region.

Renters: Section 131 of the Residential Tenancies Act already forces rent reductions on-rent controlled units when property taxes on a rental building are cut by more than 2.5%. Bradford has asked Minister Flack to extend that same mechanism to permanent municipal water rate reductions, a change with no cost to the province or to landlords. Reductions also apply to tenants of Toronto Community Housing and Toronto Seniors Housing.
