Title: Brad Bradford will eliminate Toronto's land transfer tax for the average homebuyer
Published: 2026-09-29
Issue category: Cost of Living

TORONTO, ON | Brad Bradford today announced that as mayor he will eliminate Toronto's Municipal Land Transfer Tax on the value of a principal residence below the average Toronto home price, $1.1 million in 2025. If you're buying your first home, or you're a Toronto homeowner selling one home to buy another, you would pay no Municipal Land Transfer Tax on the first $1.1 million of the purchase. On a home at the threshold, that's about $18,000 back.

“Three of four Toronto renters don’t believe they’ll ever be able to afford to buy a home here. I refuse to accept that’s the best we can do. I’m running for mayor to make life more affordable and restore the dream of homeownership,” said Bradford.

Toronto is the only city in Canada that charges its own land transfer tax on top of the province's. It's collected on closing day, in cash, on top of the down payment, legal fees and the moving truck, on a purchase that's already stretched most families to the limit. And the one bit of relief the City offers has been left to rot. The first-time buyer rebate was set at $400,000 in 2008 and hasn't been touched since. Back then it covered most homes in the city. Today it fully exempts only 2.6% of them. Bradford's plan takes that from 2.6% of homes to as many as two thirds.

"When a family or a young worker finally scrapes together a down payment on a condo, the last thing they should hear at the lawyer's office is that City Hall wants another five figures before they get the keys," said Bradford. "Over the past six years, I’ve tried four times to give first-time buyers a tax break. And every chance she had, Olivia Chow voted it down. That's status quo thinking, and it's why a generation of Torontonians is getting priced out of the city they grew up in. We're going to fix it."

The plan has two parts. First, the first-time buyer rebate is expanded from a fixed $400,000 to the previous year's average Toronto sale price, and it moves with the market every year so it never falls out of date again. Most first-time buyers in Toronto are buying condos, well under the average price, which means for most of them the municipal land transfer tax disappears entirely.

Second, a new Principal Residence Rebate extends the same break to Toronto homeowners who sell their home to buy another one in the city. A senior who no longer needs the family home can downsize without a land transfer tax penalty if they buy again in Toronto, which frees that home up for a young family. A couple who bought a condo to get their foot in the door can move up to a house when the kids arrive. Today the tax punishes both moves and keeps people stuck in homes that no longer fit – or worse, push them to buy a home outside of Toronto. This is a tax cut for people who already live here and want to stay.

It's fair, and it's targeted. Investors and landlords who own more than one Toronto property, who make up 29% of purchases in this city, don't get the exemption. Neither do corporations or commercial buyers. Existing rates continue to apply on the value above $1.1 million, and the new higher rates NDP Mayor Chow introduced on homes over $3 million stay in place. Bradford’s tax break is for people who are going to live in the home.

The tax reduction would take effect for purchases from today, subject to Council approval. Bradford is setting the date now so that nobody who's ready to buy or sell puts their life on hold for a month waiting for the election.

"This is the tax cut Toronto can actually afford, because we're paying for it with money City Hall is already collecting," said Bradford.

Under NDP Mayor Chow, the answer to affordability has been to take more. Property taxes are up nearly 20% in 3 years, including the largest increase in the city's history at 9.5%, and the likelihood of another 20% at least if she's re-elected. Higher land transfer tax rates. Water rates are up 3 years in a row. And with all of that money, 4.1 million food bank visits last year, up 340% since 2019. Olivia Chow stood on the debate stage and declared that Toronto has reached its full potential. Bradford rejects that. A city where young people can't buy a home and seniors can't afford to sell one is not a city at its full potential. We're not even close.

"Toronto isn't broken. It's stuck. Stuck with a first-time buyer rebate from 2008 that hasn’t moved a penny. Stuck with a tax that keeps seniors in homes they don't need and young families out of the ones they do," said Bradford. "We're on the cusp of change. Change is within our grasp. On October 26, Toronto can choose a city you can afford, a city that moves, and a city that feels safe. A Toronto we can be proud of. But to change the city, we need to change the mayor."

BACKGROUND: ELIMINATING THE LAND TRANSFER TAX ON HOMES BELOW THE AVERAGE PRICE

What it is: the Municipal Land Transfer Tax is eliminated on the value of an eligible principal residence below the average Toronto home price in the previous year, $1.1 million for 2025 sales. Existing rates continue to apply on value above that threshold. The rates introduced in December 2025 on homes above $3 million remain in place.

Who qualifies: first-time buyers, and existing Toronto homeowners who purchase a new principal residence in Toronto having sold their current one, or signed a firm and binding agreement of sale for it, within the previous six months (the Principal Residence Rebate). Buyers sign an affidavit, as first-time buyers do today. Co-ops and non-profits are eligible. Multi-property owners and landlords (29% of Toronto purchases), corporations and commercial transactions are not.

How many homes: more than two thirds of resale homes in Toronto sold below the 2025 average price of $1.1 million, according to TRREB data. The current first-time buyer rebate, fixed at $400,000 since 2008, fully exempts about 2.6% of homes.

What it's worth: on a home at the $1.1 million threshold, the saving is approximately $18,000.

Timing: applies to purchases with an agreement dated on or after September 29, 2026, subject to Council approval. For the Principal Residence Rebate, the sale of the existing home must also be agreed after that date, so that transactions already completed before the announcement do not receive a windfall.

Cost and funding: the exemption reduces Municipal Land Transfer Tax revenue by approximately $300 million a year. It is fully funded through Bradford’s plan outlined yesterday to modernize Toronto’s budget. This includes changes to amortize capital projects over their useful life instead of funding them from the current year’s operating budget and through dividends received from the city’s new 100% publicly-owned water utility announced yesterday.

Why the Land Transfer Tax: it penalizes moving, raises transaction costs, and locks households into homes that no longer fit. It is also volatile, rising in booms and collapsing in downturns, which makes it a poor way to fund permanent city services. The Principal Residence Rebate follows the same principle as the United Kingdom's reduced stamp duty on main residence purchases.

Brad Bradford’s Record on the Municipal Land Transfer Tax

As a councillor, Bradford has repeatedly worked to reduce costs for first-time homebuyers. This includes:

January 2020: Seconding a motion by Councillor Mark Grimes to review and increase the First-Time Home Buyers’ Rebate. (2020.MM14.6)

December 2021: Supporting an amendment by Councillor Stephen Holyday to review and increase the First-Time Home Buyers’ Rebate. (2023.CC2.1, motion 10b; passed 21-5)

September 2023: Moving an amendment to increase the First-Time Home Buyers’ Rebate to $750,000. (2023.EX7.1, motion 9b; defeated 10-13; Olivia Chow voted against)

December 2025: Moving an amendment to increase the First-Time Home Buyers’ Rebate to $800,000. (2025.EX28.1, motion 5b; defeated 4-19; Olivia Chow voted against)
