Title: Bradford to Toronto moms: This should be the best city in the world to raise a family. Right now, it doesn't feel like it
Published: 2026-10-09
Issue category: Cost of Living

TORONTO, ON | This morning in the Upper Beach, Brad Bradford sat down with a group of Toronto mothers for a kitchen-table conversation about the things that keep them up at night: the cost of staying in this city, whether the playground is safe, the hours lost in traffic that should be spent with their kids, and whether their children will ever be able to afford a home of their own here.

"Every mom at that table is doing everything right. Working, saving, driving the carpool, checking the wood chips for needles before the kids get off the slide," said Bradford. "They're holding this city together, and every year they pay more and get less for it. Toronto isn't broken. It's stuck. And the families holding it together are the ones paying the price."

The province has never given the City of Toronto more money than it has over the past 3 years. NDP Mayor Chow still raised property taxes nearly 20%, the largest increase in the city's history. If she's re-elected she'll raise them another 20% at least. In the same 3 years, food bank visits climbed to 4.1 million a year, up 340% since 2019. More than 1 in 10 of our neighbours now needs a food bank to get through the month, and plenty of them are parents. Where did the money go?

Bradford heard the same story from mothers around the table that he has heard at doors across the city. A family buys a condo to get a foot in the door. Then the second child arrives, and they need more space. Toronto is the only city in Canada that charges its own land transfer tax on top of the province's, so moving up in this city means handing City Hall tens of thousands of dollars in cash on closing day, on top of the down payment, the lawyer and the moving truck. Many families look at that bill and move out of Toronto altogether. The city loses them, their kids leave their schools and their friends, and the grandparents are an hour away.

"The double land transfer tax is a tax on growing up and a tax on growing your family," said Bradford. "As mayor, I'll eliminate Toronto's municipal land transfer tax on the value of a home below the average Toronto price, $1.1 million. That's for first-time buyers, and it's for the family that already lives here and needs a bigger place when the kids arrive. At the threshold, that's about $18,000 back. No family should have to leave the city they love because City Hall made it too expensive to stay."

Safety came up at every turn. Mothers described walking the playground before letting their kids play. Planning the route to school around the corners to avoid. Another weekend, another shooting on the news. As mayor, Bradford will take his seat on the Toronto Police Services Board, put 1,000 more front line officers on the street and a visible police presence at every subway station, go after the repeat violent offenders who are terrorizing the same neighbourhoods over and over, install platform edge doors on the subway, and bring in more voluntary and, in severe cases, mandatory treatment for people whose addiction has taken over their lives, so that parks and playgrounds belong to families again.

"A city that is not safe is not free," said Bradford. "A mom should not have to do a sweep of the sandbox before her four year old can play in it. Compassion for people struggling with addiction and safe public spaces for children are not opposites. A real city delivers both."

Then there is the time. Toronto has the worst commutes in North America, and nobody voted for that. For a working parent it means missing dinner, missing the game, missing bedtime. Bradford calls it the time tax: hours you never get back, and nobody at City Hall can tell you where they went. His plan includes a 15 minute service guarantee on the TTC and a single point of accountability for construction across the city, with published timelines and consequences when they slip.

Bradford's plan for families starts with affordability: a property tax freeze in year one, water bills cut 25%, about $300 a year for the average household, keeping Toronto's school food program so every kid is fed, and fixing the recreation registration system so parents aren't racing a crashing website at 7 in the morning for a swimming spot. All of it backed by the first full, independent, line by line review of City spending since 2011, so every dollar is accounted for before anyone at City Hall asks a family for another one.

NDP Mayor Chow stood on the debate stage and declared that Toronto has reached its full potential. Bradford rejects that. In a city where 4.1 million food bank visits a year is normal, where parents check playgrounds for needles and families move away because they can't afford to grow, we're not even close.

"I want Toronto to be the best city in the world to raise a family. Not someday. Now," said Bradford. "The moms I met this morning haven't given up on this city, and neither have I. We're on the cusp of change. Change is within our grasp. But to change the city, we need to change the mayor. On October 26, Toronto's families can choose a city they can afford, a city that moves, and a city that feels safe. A Toronto we can be proud of again."
